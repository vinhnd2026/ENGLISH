1. Countable and uncountable nouns — The basics

a) Countable nouns

a singular and a plural form · can be counted with numbers: a shirt, shirts, some shirts, four shirts

A countable noun has a singular and a plural form. We can use a singular or plural verb with it, and we can use numbers with it.

Where is my shirt?
Where are my shirts?

b) Uncountable nouns

only one form · always a singular verb · cannot be counted with numbers

An uncountable noun has only one form. We can only use a singular verb with it, and we cannot use numbers with it. However, we can count it indirectly by using a phrase like a piece of, a bit of.

Here is some advice for you.
When I left home my mother gave me two useful bits of advice.
I've found out several interesting pieces of information.

A few uncountable nouns end in -s, but they follow the normal rules for uncountable nouns and have a singular verb:

The news is very bad today.
Billiards is an interesting game.

2. Typical uncountable nouns

Substances: water, air, coffee, plastic, iron, paper  ·  Abstract ideas: life, fun, freedom, health, time, progress  ·  Activities: work, travel, sleep, football, help, research  ·  Human feelings: happiness, anger, honesty, hope, respect, courage  ·  Groups of items: furniture, luggage

Note the words which are uncountable in English but countable in many other languages: accommodation, advice, behaviour, business, cash, equipment, furniture, health, homework, information, knowledge, luggage, money, permission, rubbish, scenery, traffic, travel, weather, work.

3. Category use and changes of meaning

Some uncountable nouns can be used in a countable way when they describe a category:

Would you like some fruit after your coffee?
There are two main fruits exported from Madeira, bananas and pineapples.

Some words have different meanings in countable and uncountable forms:

  • an iron (a domestic appliance) ↔ some iron (a substance/material); a wood (a small area of trees) ↔ some wood (material); a paper (a newspaper) ↔ some paper (material); a chicken (the animal) ↔ chicken (the meat)
  • a coffee = a cup of coffee ↔ coffee = the substance
  • business (in general) ↔ a business (a company); gossip (talking) ↔ a gossip (a person); hair (all together) ↔ a hair (a single strand); help (in general) ↔ a help (a helpful person/thing); toast (grilled bread) ↔ a toast (formal words before drinking); work (in general) ↔ a work (a work of art/engineering)

4. Plural-only nouns and group nouns

a) Plural nouns

trousers, stairs, clothes, contents, feelings, goods, jeans, means, outskirts, surroundings, thanks + plural verb

These nouns only have a plural form and take a plural verb.

My trousers are too tight.
The stairs are very steep.

b) Group nouns

government, army, audience, class, company, crew, crowd, family, group, media, press, public, staff, team + singular or plural verb

Use a singular verb when we think of the group as a whole, and a plural verb when we think of its individual members. Some group nouns only take a plural verb: cattle, police, people.

I think the government is/are wrong.
The police are arriving shortly.

⭐ Key points

  1. Check in a dictionary to be sure that a noun is countable or uncountable.
  2. The meaning of a noun may change depending on whether it is used in a countable or uncountable sense (a paper = a newspaper; paper = the material).
  3. Some nouns ending in -s take a singular verb: The news is bad today.
  4. Some nouns only have a plural form and take a plural verb: The police are arriving shortly., trousers, stairs, clothes.
  5. To count an uncountable noun, use an indirect phrase: a piece of advice/information, a slice of toast, a glass of milk.
