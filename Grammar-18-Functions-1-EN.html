1. What are functions?

When we study English, we can describe a sentence in two ways. A grammatical description tells us about the structure of the sentence, while a functional description tells us about the purpose of the sentence — what the speaker uses it to do: ask permission, invite, advise, apologise…

The same sentence: grammatical description ≠ functional description
If I were you, I'd leave now.
— Grammatically this is a second conditional; functionally, this sentence is used for giving advice.

The three sentences below are all used for asking permission, but they differ in politeness:

Is it all right if I use the phone?
— Said to friends: informal and relaxed.
Do you think I could possibly phone Australia?
— Still to someone you know, but the matter is more serious, so the wording is more formal and polite.
Do you mind if I open the window?
— Said to a stranger: the polite form.

2. Choosing how to say it, according to the situation

How we choose our words is influenced by the situation, by the person we are talking to and by the subject of the conversation. There are no rigid rules, but we usually use polite, formal wording when:

  • talking to a stranger, or someone we do not know well;
  • talking to someone of higher status;
  • discussing sensitive subjects.

With close friends, on the other hand, informal, shorter wording usually sounds more natural. Choosing the wrong level of politeness can make the listener uncomfortable, or make us seem rude.

Shall I carry this bag for you?
— Offering to do something.
Would you like to come round for a drink later?
— Inviting.
Could you tell me how to get to the post office?
— Asking for directions.
What do you think I should do?
— Asking for advice.

3. The functions practised in this lesson

Here are the communicative functions practised in this lesson, with a typical sentence for each:

  • Asking for and giving advice: I think you'd better phone the police.
  • Agreeing and disagreeing: I think that's the point exactly. / Actually, I don't think that's right.
  • Apologising: I'm sorry I'm late — it won't happen again.
  • Complaining: You might have told me you were having a party!
  • Ending a conversation: Well, it's been nice talking to you, but I'm afraid I have to go.
  • Asking for and giving directions: It's at the end of this street, opposite the church.
  • Greeting; Asking how someone is: Hi, Sally, how are you?
  • Asking for information: Could you tell me what time the bank opens?
  • Introducing yourself and others: Jack, this is my brother, Mark.
  • Inviting; Accepting and declining invitations: That's very kind of you, I'd love to. / I'd love to come, but I'm already going out that evening.
  • Offering something: Would you like some more tea?
  • Offering to do something: Would you like me to do the washing-up?
Good morning, I'm Brenda Watson, the Marketing Manager.
— Introducing yourself, in a formal context.
Excuse me, but is the bus station anywhere near here?
— Asking a stranger for directions, opening with "Excuse me" to be polite.

⭐ Key points

  1. How we choose our words depends on the situation, on our relationship with the person we are talking to, and on the subject under discussion.
  2. We need to respond appropriately — informally or politely — depending on those factors, and on how the other person spoke to us first.
  3. If we fail to use a polite form when one is needed, the listener may think we are rude, and we may not get the result we want from the conversation.
