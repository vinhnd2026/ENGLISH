1. Books, theatre and film

Books: a chapter is one of the main divisions of a book; a preface is the introduction at the front; the index at the back tells you where things are mentioned; the cover is the outside; to publish is to produce and sell a book; a best seller sells in very large numbers; a paperback has a soft cover; reviews are critics' opinions in newspapers.

Theatre and film: a scene is one part of a play or film; an act is a larger division; the interval is the break in the middle; films are shown on a screen, plays are performed on a stage; actors wear costumes; a sketch is a rough drawing; a painting has a frame round it; a portrait is a picture of a person; a close up shows faces from very near; the foreground is the front part of a picture.

Music: an orchestra is a large group of classical musicians; a band / group plays popular music; a choir is a group of singers; live means performed in front of an audience, not recorded.

Her second book was very popular and became a best seller.
No recording can be as good as a live concert.
live (pronounced /laɪv/) = performed in front of an audience, not recorded.
The average viewer watches television for about 15 hours a week.

2. People in the arts

Music: the conductor directs an orchestra; the composer writes the music. Writing: an author writes books; a novelist writes novels; a playwright writes plays; an editor decides what a newspaper or book contains; a critic writes reviews.

Stage, film and television: the director directs a play or film; an announcer introduces programmes; a sculptor makes statues and other objects in metal, stone or wood; the cast is all the actors in a play or film.

Watching: a viewer watches television; an audience watches a play, film or concert; a spectator watches an event, especially a sport.

Charles Dickens is probably the best known British novelist.
Peter sings every Sunday in the local church choir.

3. Describing works, phrasal verbs and compounds

Describing words: live (not recorded), humorous (funny), gripping (so exciting you cannot stop), dull (boring), readable (easy and enjoyable to read), and the fixed partnerships modern art, current affairs, popular music, special effects, public library, still life (a painting of objects) and electric guitar.

Phrasal verbs: pick up — receive (a radio station); put on — stage (a play); call off — cancel; look up — search for information; turn up — make louder; come out — be published or released; go over — study carefully; take over — take control of (a company); work out — find the answer to; take up — start (a hobby).

Compounds: masterpiece, soap opera (a long-running TV drama), autobiography (the story of your own life), dress rehearsal (the final practice in costume), paperback, dress circle (expensive theatre seats), foreground, film scripts, library book and funfair.

I couldn't put that book down, it had such a gripping plot.
gripping = so exciting that you cannot stop reading or watching.
The school put on Hamlet in modern dress.
put on = stage a play; in modern dress = with modern costumes.
Colin's new book comes out next week.
come out = be published or released.
Jim has taken up painting as a hobby.
take up = start a new hobby or activity.
We had very good seats in the dress circle.

⭐ Key points

  1. Distinguish viewer (television), audience (a play, film or concert) and spectator (an event, especially sport).
  2. Fixed prepositional phrases: at work, out of tune, in the background, in paperback, on (Channel 4), in modern dress, out of date, on stage.
  3. publish a book — not broadcast it; a best seller, not a best buy.
  4. A scene is part of a film or play, an act is a larger division, and the interval is the break; classical music is performed by an orchestra, popular music by a band or group.
  5. come out = be released; take up = start a hobby; put on = stage a play; work out = find an answer by thinking.
  6. Watch the spellings: imaginative (from imagine), accompanied (from accompany), scenery (from scene); a self portrait is a picture of yourself.
