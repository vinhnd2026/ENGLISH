1. What is a phrasal verb?

Verbs are often followed by a particle (an adverb or a preposition) such as back, off, through, up. Sometimes the verb and the particle keep their normal meanings; at other times, put together, they create a completely new meaning. Compare:

Can you bring up the radio from downstairs?
(normal meaning: carry something upstairs)
She has brought up two children on her own.
(new meaning: look after a child until they are adult)

The term phrasal verb is used for the second case: verb + particle together have a special meaning. Phrasal verbs are very common in informal English.

One phrasal verb can have several different meanings — the correct one is only clear from the context.

There are four types of phrasal verbs, covered in Grammar 26 and 27. Grammar 26 looks at: (1) verbs with two particles; (2) verbs with one particle which need an object and are inseparable.

2. Verbs with two particles

Most of the verbs in this list need an object, and the object can only come at the end — so the verbs are inseparable:

V + particle + particle + O
I’m looking forward to my holidays.

Verbs marked with an asterisk * have another form: there is no object, and the final particle is not used:

I’ve decided to cut down on smoking. — I’ve decided to cut down.
  • cut down on* — reduce the amount of
  • catch up with* — reach the same place as
  • come up against — meet, find (a problem)
  • come up with — think of
  • drop in on* — visit for a short time
  • face up to — accept, deal with
  • feel up to — have the strength and energy to do
  • get away with — do something bad and not be punished
  • get along/on with* — have good relations with
  • get on with — continue with
  • get out of — avoid a responsibility
  • get round to — find time to do
  • get up to — do something bad
  • go in for — have as a hobby
  • grow out of — become too old for
  • keep up with* — stay in the same place as
  • look down on — feel superior to
  • look up to — respect
  • look forward to — think we will enjoy
  • make up for — compensate for
  • put up with — accept without complaining
  • run out of* — have no more of
  • stand up for — defend
They are too far ahead for us to catch up with them.
Have you come up with an answer yet?
Jack stole the money and got away with it.
I can’t put up with these screaming children!
Oh dear, we’ve run out of petrol!
You must learn to stand up for yourself!

3. Verbs with one particle: transitive and inseparable

These phrasal verbs take an object, and the object must come after the particle. It cannot go between the verb and the particle:

V + particle + O
I love coffee. I can’t do without it in the morning!
(NOT “do it without”)
  • ask after — ask for news of
  • call for — come to your house and collect you
  • call on — visit for a short time
  • come across — find by chance
  • come into — inherit
  • count on — depend on
  • deal with — take action to solve a problem
  • do without — manage without having
  • get at — suggest
  • get over — recover from
  • go over — discuss the details
  • join in — take part in, contribute to
  • live on — have as income
  • look into — investigate
  • look round — look at everything
  • make for — go in the direction of
  • pick on — choose a person to punish
  • run into — meet by chance
  • see about — make arrangements
  • see to — attend to, take care of
  • stand for — tolerate; be a candidate for
  • take after — have the same characteristics as
Joe came across this old painting in the attic.
Sue came into a large sum of money.
I’m counting on you to help me.
Helen takes after her mother.

⭐ Key points

  1. When you meet a new phrasal verb, check it in a dictionary to see how it is used.
  2. Many phrasal verbs have more than one meaning — use the context to choose the right one.
  3. Phrasal verbs tend to be more common in spoken language and informal writing than in formal writing.
