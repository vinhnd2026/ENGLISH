Grammar 13 — Linking Words 1

Linking words: purpose · result · contrast — so that · to · such · too / enough · although · despite

1. Purpose

a) So that

S + V … + so that + S + can / could / will / would + V

So that is followed by a full clause and expresses purpose. It is the most common way of showing purpose, especially in speech.

I'll give you a key so that you can let yourself in.
I gave you a key so that you could let yourself in.
(In the past, could is used.)

If the subject of the main clause and the subject of the purpose clause are the same, we can also use the to-infinitive. If they are different, you must use so that.

I went home to change. / I went home so that I could change.
(Same subject, so both forms are fine.)
I left her a message so that she would know where to go.
(Different subjects, so only so that works.)

b) In order to and so as to

These two phrases are more formal than the plain to-infinitive. The negative forms are in order not to / so as not to.

We moved house in order to be nearer the office.
He exercises every day so as not to put on weight.

c) For + verb-ing (the function of a thing)

To explain the purpose or function of an object, use for + V-ing.

This tool is for opening tins.

2. Result

a) So … that and such (a) … that

so + adjective / adverb + that  ·  such (a/an) + (adjective) + noun + that

The that-clause gives the result. Notice: so goes before an adjective or adverb; such goes before a (adjective +) noun.

The music was so loud that you could hear it from miles away.
They had such an exciting holiday that they have talked about nothing else.

b) So with many / much / few / little

Before a quantifier, use so (not such).

We had so much work that we didn't go out. / There was so little time that we had to hurry.

c) Too and not … enough

(not) + adjective / adverb + enough + to V  ·  too + adjective / adverb + to V
The coffee is too hot to drink. / He isn't old enough to drive.

3. Contrast

a) Although, though and even though

Although / Though / Even though + clause, …

All three are followed by a clause and introduce a contrast. Even though gives a stronger contrast than although; though is common in speech.

Although it was late, we went out. / Even though it was late, we went out.

b) While and whereas

These two introduce a contrast between two parallel clauses; they often stand in the middle of the sentence.

While many people spend the whole weekend shopping, others spend them relaxing.

c) However and nevertheless (formal)

Clause 1. However, + clause 2.

However / Nevertheless link two separate sentences; the second sentence usually begins with the linking word followed by a comma. They are fairly formal.

She was nervous. However, she performed well.

d) Despite and in spite of

despite / in spite of + noun or V-ing  (never a full clause)
Despite the cold weather, we all went for a walk. / In spite of being tired, she kept working.

⭐ Key points

  1. so that + a full clause shows purpose; when the two clauses have the same subject, the to-infinitive is also possible; in order to / so as to are more formal.
  2. for + V-ing explains what a thing is used for: It's for painting things.
  3. so + adjective/adverb that…; such (a) + (adjective) + noun that… — do not mix the two forms up.
  4. With many/much/few/little, use so: so many people, so little time.
  5. too + adjective + to V = “more than wanted, so it cannot be done”; (not) … enough + to V looks at whether something is sufficient.
  6. although / though / even though + clause; even though = a stronger contrast; while / whereas contrasts two parallel facts.
  7. despite / in spite of + noun or V-ing — never followed by a full clause.
