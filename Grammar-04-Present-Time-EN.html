1. The Present Simple

a) Permanent facts and truths

S + V / V-s,es  ·  takes, flows, work…

Used for facts that are always true and permanent — common in scientific and geographical descriptions, and for situations that are generally true in everyday life.

The light from the Sun takes 8 mins 20 secs to reach the Earth.
The River Po flows into the Adriatic Sea.
I work in an office and live in a flat in the suburbs.

b) Habits and repeated actions

Used for habits and routines; it often goes with an adverb of frequency (usually, always, often…).

I usually take the bus to work.

c) Summaries of events

It can be used to summarise the events of a story, a film or a book, or in a chronological table of historical events.

In Chapter 1, Susan meets David, and agrees to go to the school dance with him.
In 1789 the French Revolution begins.

2. The Present Continuous

S + am / is / are + V-ing

a) Actions in progress, not finished

Used for actions that are temporary and unfinished — happening at the moment of speaking, or around the present time.

I'm doing the washing-up.
I'm reading one of the Harry Potter books at the moment.

b) Temporary habits and repeated actions over a short period

It can also describe a habit that lasts only for a short period, or a single repeated action — in that case a time expression is needed. Used with always, it often has an exaggerated or complaining tone.

At the moment we're sending all the mail by courier, because the Post Office is on strike.
Whenever I see Tom he's smoking.
You're always borrowing money from me!
(a complaint)

3. State verbs — a common point of confusion

a) Verbs not normally used in the continuous form

Some verbs describe states that already last over a period of time, so they are not normally used in the continuous form. These are called state verbs:

be, believe, cost, depend, have, hear, know, matter, smell, suppose, taste, think, understand

b) A change of meaning in the continuous form

A few of the verbs above can be used in the continuous form — but then their meaning changes to an “action” meaning:

Tim is being rather difficult at the moment.
(be = behave: how someone acts)
I'm having breakfast.
(have = eat)
I'm tasting the soup, to check if it needs more salt.
(taste = sample, try the flavour of)
I'm thinking of buying a new car.
(think = consider)

c) Simple or continuous: permanent or temporary?

Many situations can use both forms: the simple form for a permanent situation or general habit, the continuous form for a temporary one.

I live in London.
(that is my permanent home)
I'm living in London.
(just for a year — my home is in Athens)
Do you sleep a lot?
(asking about a habit)
Are you sleeping enough?
(asking about the present situation)

⭐ Key points

  1. The Present Simple describes facts and habitual actions. The Present Continuous describes an action still in progress at the time of speaking.
  2. Many verbs describing a state (not a momentary event) are used only in the simple form. Verbs of mental activity such as understand, know belong to this group.
  3. Some verbs have both a state meaning and an event meaning, but the two meanings are not the same.
  4. When we describe a photograph, we usually describe the scene as if it were happening right now → use the Present Continuous.
  5. Present forms are also used to talk about future time (see Grammar 3 — Future time).
  6. There are cases where English uses the Present Perfect where other languages use a present tense (see Grammar 2): I've lived in Milan all my life.
