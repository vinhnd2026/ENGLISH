1. The five light verbs: do · make · have · take · give

These five very common verbs combine with many different nouns to form fixed combinations (collocations). The meaning belongs to the whole combination, not to each word separately — learn each pair as a unit.

do: do well · do your best · do everything you can
make: make a suggestion · make a mess · make an exception
have: have the fish · have no idea · have a good sense of humour
take: take a pill · take a size 10 · take the bus
give: give someone a lift · give someone a headache · give priority to something

A useful guide: do goes with work, tasks and effort; make goes with producing a new result or change.

Just do your best — it doesn't matter if you don't win.
We don't normally give refunds, but in this case we'll make an exception.
I'm sorry, I have absolutely no idea.

2. Verb + noun

Many verbs combine with one fixed noun only. Replacing the verb with a near-synonym makes the phrase sound wrong, even if the meaning seems close.

shake your head · suit your mood · lose your patience · tackle a problem · reach a target · withdraw money · enrol on a course · waste a chance
Stay calm, don't lose your patience — it's nearly finished.
He gets an extra bonus every month if he can reach his target.
I knew he disagreed when I saw him shake his head.

3. Adjective + noun and noun + noun

A noun collocates with only a limited set of adjectives; two nouns can also combine in a fixed pair to make one noun phrase.

a tough challenge · wide support · a disturbing trend · body language · an ideal opportunity · true love · minor damage · a live recording
sales staff · market leader · visitor centre · crash landing · rice production · rubbish collection · skills shortage · holiday weekend
Coke sells more than Pepsi in my country. It's the market leader.
The plane was out of control and had to make a crash landing.
We're facing a serious skills shortage in our hospitals.

4. Adverb + verb / adverb + adjective

Adverbs have fixed partners too: an adverb may collocate with one verb or adjective but not with another, even when the meanings look similar.

talk openly · search thoroughly · rain heavily · feel strongly · focus mainly
largely created · absolutely superb · seriously overweight · virtually destroyed · eternally grateful · radically changed · ridiculously overpriced · blissfully happy
It was raining heavily all day.
She feels strongly about the rights of people in poor countries.
This chocolate mousse is absolutely superb!

5. Verb phrases with to, and idioms

Many adjectives and verbs form fixed phrases of the shape … to + verb. Idioms are completely fixed expressions — learn the whole phrase as one vocabulary item.

try to · slow to · due to · hard to · happy to · asked to · dare to · allowed to · manage to · tend to
out of the blue · the rat race · chalk and cheese · in the red · pros and cons · head first · a piece of cake · on the cards · get on someone's nerves · in a nutshell
Candidates are not allowed to use dictionaries in the exam.
I just didn't see the other car. It came out of the blue.

⭐ Key points

  1. A collocation is a pair or group of words that habitually go together; learn the whole phrase as one vocabulary item, not word by word.
  2. do goes with work and effort (do well, do your best); make goes with producing a result (make a suggestion, make an exception).
  3. have / take / give each have their own set of nouns: have no idea, take the bus, give someone a lift, give priority to.
  4. Adjective + noun and noun + noun pairs are strongly fixed: body language, market leader, crash landing, skills shortage.
  5. Adverb + adjective pairs cannot be swapped freely: absolutely superb, seriously overweight, blissfully happy.
  6. Idioms are completely fixed expressions: out of the blue, in a nutshell, a piece of cake, on the cards.
