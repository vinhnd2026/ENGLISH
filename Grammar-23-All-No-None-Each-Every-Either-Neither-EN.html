Grammar 23 — All, no, none, each, every, either, neither

Quantity and choice: all · no · none · each · every · either · neither

1. All · No · None

a) All — the whole quantity

Use all (of) + noun to talk about the total quantity. For emphasis, all can come after the subject.

Jim was there all the time.
They all wore white shorts and shirts.

All + subject + verb means “the only thing”: All I want is some peace and quiet.

⚠️ Do not use all on its own as a subject or object — use everything: Everything has gone wrong! (not *All has gone wrong!)

b) No — equals “not any”

No + noun gives a complete negative. Use no before comparatives: no worse than, no less than 500. No is not normally used on its own before an adjective.

There are no plates left.
There were no less than 500 applications for the job.

Idiomatic uses: no good (= useless); no + verb-ing in notices: No smoking.

c) None — not one / not any of them

We do not say *no of — use none of, or none on its own. The verb after none is usually plural in everyday speech and singular in formal writing. For emphasis: none at all / not one.

None of the films that are showing in town look very interesting.
How many people came to the party? — None at all!

2. Each · Every

Each and every are similar in meaning and are often interchangeable. Both are used with a singular noun and a singular verb.

Each time I come here, I go to my favourite restaurant.

The difference

Each looks at items one by one; every looks at them together as a group. So each suits smaller numbers — even just two; every is used for larger numbers and for repeated actions (like every day).

They gave a medal to each member of the team.
I believe every word he says.
She kissed him on each cheek.
⚠️ We say each of, but not *every of: Each of them was given a medal. Each can also come after the subject or at the end of the sentence: The members each received a medal. / The members received a medal each.

3. Either · Neither

Either and neither talk about two things or people: either = one or the other (it does not matter which), neither = not one and not the other. Both are used with singular nouns and verbs.

Monday or Tuesday? Yes, either day is fine.
I'm sorry, but neither day is convenient.

Not … either has the same meaning as neither. Either can also mean “both”, as in on either side.

I didn't like either of those films.
On either side of the house there are shops.

⭐ Key points

  1. All + subject + verb means “the only thing”: All we need now is a new car. But do not use all on its own as a subject — use everything: Everything is missing, I'm afraid.
  2. No is used idiomatically in notices and fixed phrases: No parking. No smoking. It's no use. It's no good.
  3. Each looks at items one by one; every looks at the group together and is used for larger numbers. Both take a singular verb. We say each of but not *every of: Each of these books has its interesting points.
  4. Either and neither refer to two items separately and take a singular verb: Neither of these hotels is very comfortable. Either one would be OK.
  5. After none, the verb is often plural in everyday speech and singular in formal writing: None of the students have/has answered the question correctly.
