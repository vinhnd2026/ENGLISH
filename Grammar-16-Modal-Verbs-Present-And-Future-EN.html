Grammar 16 — Modal Verbs: Present and Future

Modal verbs — present and future: can / be able to · must / can't · may / might / could · should / ought to · have to · had better · be bound to

1. Ability

Can and be able to

can + V  ·  be able to + V

Can, like all modal verbs, has no infinitive form and no continuous form. When those forms are needed, use be able to instead.

I'd like to be able to swim.
(the infinitive form: to be able to)
Not being able to swim is annoying.
(the -ing form: being able to)

2. Deduction: certainty and uncertainty

a) Must and can't — logical deduction

must be … (certain that it is true)  ·  can't be … (certain that it is impossible)

These two are used for deduction: we are sure, or almost sure, about something because it is logical. This use is particularly common with the verb be. (Must is also used for obligation in section 3, and can/can't for ability in section 1.)

You must be tired after your long journey.
(I am sure you are tired)
That can't be Sue. She's in Brazil.
(I am sure it is not her)

b) May, might and could — possible (about 50%)

All three express uncertainty — a possibility of about 50%. In this sense, could is not used with not. In speech, strong stress on the modal verb makes the possibility even smaller.

I might go out, I don't know. I could get wet!
I might see you later if I finish my work, but don't count on it.
(a heavily stressed might → a small possibility)

c) Should and ought to — expecting something to happen

As well as obligation, should / ought to can be used when we expect something to happen.

Brenda should be home by now.
(I expect she has arrived home)

d) Be bound to — certain to happen

be bound to + V

Used to say that something is certain to happen in the future.

You're bound to see Paula if you go there.
(I am sure you will see her)

3. Obligation

a) Must and have to

In writing, the two are almost identical. In speech there is sometimes a small difference: must when the speaker personally feels that something is important; have to when outside circumstances make it necessary.

You must start working harder! — You have to turn left here.
(the speaker insists) — (an outside rule requires it)

b) Mustn't and don't have to — completely different!

mustn't = forbidden (not allowed to do it)  ·  don't have to = not necessary (the choice is yours)

Must and have to are very similar in the affirmative, but in the negative they are completely different: mustn't means something is forbidden; don't have to means something is not necessary.

You mustn't leave now.
(against the rules)
You don't have to leave now.
(the choice is yours)

c) Should and ought to — what is best, advice

These two are synonyms, used to say what is best to do: giving opinions, advice, or polite instructions.

I think you should see a doctor.
You ought not to smoke if you're pregnant.

d) Had better — strong advice to avoid trouble

had better (+ not) + V

This phrase gives strong advice about what to do right now in order to stop something bad from happening; it is used for the present or the future.

You'd better not drive.
(it could be dangerous)

e) Is/Are to — formal instructions

be to + V (negative: be not to — the word not is stressed)

Used in formal instructions.

No-one is to leave the room.

⭐ Key points

  1. Most modal verbs (modal auxiliaries) have more than one meaning. You need the context — or the speaker's tone of voice — to understand which meaning is intended.
  2. The negative forms mustn't and don't have to are completely different: You mustn't go. (forbidden — against the rules) · You don't have to go. (not necessary).
  3. In ordinary speech, should expresses a weaker obligation than must and have to: You should go to the doctor. (in my opinion, a good idea) · You must go to the doctor. (really necessary). But in formal speech and writing, should can express a strong obligation: Passengers for Gatwick Airport should change at Reading.
