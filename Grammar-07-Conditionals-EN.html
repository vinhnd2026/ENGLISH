Grammar 7 — Conditionals

Conditionals: first · second · third · mixed · unless, provided, as long as · should · were to

1. Real or possible conditions — First conditional

If + present simple,  will / won't + V

The first conditional describes situations that are real or possible. After if we use a present tense, but the time referred to is the future; the result clause usually uses will / won't, which can be replaced by going to or can.

If you fall, I won't be able to catch you!
(a real possibility)
If it rains, we're going to get wet.
(going to instead of will)
If the cases are too heavy, I can help you carry them.
(can in the result clause)

Unless, provided, as long as

Unless means If … not. Provided and as long as can also introduce a condition.

Unless you leave at once, I'll call the police. (= If you don't leave at once, I'll call the police.)
Provided you leave now, you'll catch the train.

Imperatives instead of a result clause; should instead of if

An imperative is often used instead of an if-clause: Get me some cigarettes, and I'll pay you later. We can also use should instead of if, meaning “if by any chance…” — this makes the event sound less likely.

Should you see John, can you give him a message?

2. Unreal conditions in the present or future — Second conditional

If + past simple,  would + V

The second conditional describes situations that are unreal or imaginary. After if we use the past simple, but the time referred to is still the future; the result clause usually uses would, or might / could when we are less certain.

If you fell, you would hurt yourself.
(an imaginary situation)
If you became a millionaire, you might be unhappy.
(might = a less certain prediction)

Were and were to

In formal language, were is often used instead of was for all persons. Were to is another way of expressing a second conditional.

If I were taller, I'd join the basketball team. · If I were you, I'd leave now.
If they were to offer me the job, I'd turn it down.

3. Unreal conditions in the past — Third conditional & mixed conditions

If + past perfect,  would have + V₃

The third conditional describes situations that are unreal in the past: the speaker imagines a past different from what actually happened. Might / could are also common in the result clause.

If John had studied more, he would have got better marks.
(in reality, he did not)
If you had tried harder, you might have succeeded.

Mixed conditions

For a past event whose result continues into the present, we can use a third conditional form in the if-clause and a second conditional form in the result clause.

If you had saved some money, you wouldn't be so hard up.

4. Other uses of if

If can mean when / whenever — this kind of sentence uses the present simple in both clauses. If can also mean “if it is true that…”. And if + past simple is used for past events that are possible or known to be true, with no special rule.

If/When/Whenever it rains, we play football indoors instead.
If you missed the TV programme last night, you can borrow my recording.

⭐ Key points

  1. The present form in a first conditional does not refer to present time — it refers to the future.
  2. The past form in a second conditional does not refer to past time — it also refers to the future.
  3. The difference between the first and second conditional can depend on the speaker's attitude: a high chance of happening → first conditional; a low chance / imaginary → second conditional. If she falls, she'll land in the safety net. (she really could fall) compared with If she fell, she would land in the safety net. (only an imagined situation).
  4. Might and could often appear in conditionals when we are not sure about a prediction.
  5. We can use a mixed conditional, especially when a past event has a result in the present: If Brenda hadn't stolen the money, she wouldn't be in prison.
  6. Unless, provided and as long as can all introduce a condition.
