Grammar 2 — Present Perfect

The present perfect: Present Perfect Simple · Present Perfect Continuous · for / since / ago

1. The Present Perfect Simple

S + have / has + V₃ (past participle)

a) Recent events

Used to describe events that have happened recently. The event took place in the past, but its result is still in the present. No definite time is given; to emphasise recentness, use just, and use yet for something that has not happened.

I've left my shopping bag behind.
I've broken my arm, as you can see.
(the result is still plainly visible now)
I've just broken my watch.
I haven't found her phone number yet.

b) Indefinite events

No definite time is given: these are events in our life experience up to now. After It's/This is the first/second time… we also use the present perfect.

I've been to France three times.
(in my life up to now — no definite time is stated)
This is the first time I have eaten Japanese food.

c) Contrast with the Past Simple

Events described with the Past Simple always have a definite time. Even mentioning a definite place for an event can suggest a definite time → use the Past Simple.

I went to France last year. I ate at a Japanese restaurant on Saturday.
(with definite time expressions: last year, on Saturday)
I left my shopping bag on the train.
(a definite place is mentioned, suggesting a definite time)

2. Events that last or repeat up to the present

a) Present Perfect Simple — a lasting state

It describes a state that lasts up to the present, or a habitual action in a period of time up to now.

I've lived in this house for five years.
(and I am still living here)
I've never worn a tie to work, and I refuse to start now!

b) Present Perfect Continuous — an action in progress

S + have / has + been + V-ing

It also describes a state lasting up to the present; with this group, there is little difference in meaning between the simple and continuous forms, including in How long…? questions. Some verbs, such as wait, sit, lie, stay, prefer the continuous form.

I've been living in this house for five years.
How long have you lived in this house? — How long have you been living in this house?
(the two questions are equivalent)
I've been waiting for ages.
(the continuous form is preferred with wait)

3. Simple or continuous?

a) Completed action → the simple form

The simple form emphasises that the action is complete. When we say how many / how much, we always use the simple form.

I've finished my homework!
I've written ten pages of my homework!
(an amount is stated → the simple form)

b) Not completed, or just stopped → the continuous form

The continuous form shows that the action is not complete, or that it has finished only recently.

We've been walking for hours! Let's have a rest.
I've been digging the garden. That's why I'm so dirty!
(the activity has just been going on; its traces are still visible)

c) Focus on the result, or on the activity?

Focus on the present result → simple form. Focus on the action in progress → continuous form.

I've written my homework. Now I can watch the television.
(focus on the result)
I've been writing my homework all evening! I didn't know it would take so long.
(focus on the activity in progress)
⚠️ Three time expressions that are often confused: for + a period of time (for five years), since + the starting point (since 2020), and ago, used only with the past simple for finished events (two days ago).

⭐ Key points

  1. The Present Perfect Simple describes events with no definite time: either the event lies in a period of time leading up to the present, or its result is still evident in the present. The choice between the present perfect and the past simple depends on how the speaker views the event: I've had an accident (still connected with the present) compared with I had an accident (viewed as finished, separate from the present).
  2. Events described with the present perfect may be recent, or not — they do not have to have happened just now.
  3. The Present Perfect Continuous is used for an action in progress: it suggests the action is unfinished or has only just finished. Compare: I've read this book (completion emphasised) — I've been reading this book (the action itself emphasised; it may not be complete). The continuous form also emphasises the length of time of the action.
  4. For refers to a period of time (finished or unfinished): I waited for three hours. He's been sitting there for ages.
  5. Since refers to the point at which an unfinished period of time began: He's been sitting there since two o'clock.
  6. Ago refers to the time of a finished event (used with the past simple): Jill arrived a week ago.
