1. Relative clauses — the basics

a) The relative pronoun as subject or object

A relative clause adds information about a noun in the main clause. It begins with a relative pronoun: who, which, that, whom, whose. The relative pronoun can be the subject of the relative clause:

That's the woman who bought my car.
(The woman is the subject of bought.)

Or it can be the object of the relative clause:

That's the car that I used to own.
(I is the subject; the car is the object.)

When two sentences are joined into one, the noun replaced by the relative pronoun is not repeated inside the relative clause: This is Jean. She bought my car. → Jean is the person who bought my car. (do not repeat she); That is Jean's car. I used to own it. → That's the car that I used to own. (do not repeat it).

b) Defining and non-defining clauses

A defining clause gives essential information that identifies exactly which person or thing is meant; without it, the meaning may be unclear. It has no commas:

That book which you lent me is really good.
(It tells us exactly which book.)

A non-defining clause only adds extra information; in writing it is separated by commas, and in speech there is a pause at both ends of the clause. If you leave the clause out, the meaning is still clear:

The book, which I hadn't read, was still on the shelf.

c) Omitting the relative pronoun

In a defining clause, if the relative pronoun is the object, it can be left out:

That's the car (that) I used to own.
(that is the object, so it can be omitted.)

But if the pronoun is the subject, it cannot be omitted (That's the woman who bought my car — you cannot drop who). In a non-defining clause, the relative pronoun can never be omitted.

2. Which, who, that, whom and whose

a) That instead of which and who

For things, that is very often used instead of which, especially in speech:

Is this the house that you bought?

For people, that can also replace who — less common, but still found, particularly in speech:

Have you met the boy that Sue is going to marry?

b) Two limits on that

That cannot begin a non-defining clause — there you must use which/who:

The hotel, which was a hundred years old, was very comfortable.
(Do not use that after the comma.)

That also cannot come immediately after a preposition. In speech, we put the preposition at the end of the clause; in formal writing, the preposition goes before which:

This is the car (that/which) I paid £2000 for. — This is the car for which I paid £2000.
(The second version is more formal.)

c) Whom and whose

Whom is the object form of who; it is required after a preposition, but it is formal and quite rare. In speech, people usually move the preposition to the end of the clause and use who/that, or leave the pronoun out:

This is the person to whom I sold my car. — This is the person (who) I sold my car to.
(The first version is formal; the second is spoken style.)

Whose means “of whom / of which” (possession), and is mostly used for people — join two sentences like This is Jack. His sister is staying with us.:

This is Jack, whose sister is staying with us.

⭐ Key points

  1. A relative pronoun that is the subject cannot be omitted; one that is the object in a defining clause can be omitted.
  2. Defining clauses: no commas, essential information. Non-defining clauses: commas, extra information only, and the relative pronoun cannot be omitted.
  3. that often replaces which (things) and sometimes who (people) in speech; but that cannot begin a non-defining clause and cannot come directly after a preposition.
  4. whom is the formal object form of who, used after a preposition (to whom…); speech usually puts the preposition at the end of the clause.
  5. whose shows possession (“of …”): the woman whose jewels were stolen…
  6. Long sentences with relative clauses are more common in writing; speech often splits them into short sentences, omits object pronouns and leaves the preposition at the end of the clause.
