Grammar 1 — Past Time

Past tenses: Past Simple · Past Continuous · Past Perfect · Used to · Would · Participle clauses

1. Talking about past events

a) Past Simple — main events, finished

S + V-ed / V₂  ·  went, bought, saw…

Used for actions and events that are complete in the past — this is the tense that tells the main events of a story.

Susan went into the station and bought a ticket.

b) Past Continuous — background / actions in progress

S + was / were + V-ing

Used for an action that was in progress at a past moment, often to describe the scene around the main event.

There were a lot of people waiting in the station. Some were sleeping on the benches, and others were walking up and down.
Susan was looking for Graham, so she didn't sit down.

A common pattern: past continuous (background, longer) + past simple (a sudden, shorter event that interrupts it):

While Susan was trying to get onto the platform, a man grabbed her handbag.

c) Past Perfect — the “past of the past”

S + had + V₃ (past participle)

When there are two past events, use the Past Perfect for the one that happened first, so the order is clear.

By the time the train arrived, Susan had managed to push her way to the front of the crowd.
Note the order: Susan reached the front of the crowd first; the train arrived second.

If before / after already makes the order clear, the Past Perfect is not required:

Before the train arrived, Susan managed to push her way to the front of the crowd.
⚠️ Do not use the Past Perfect just because something happened “a long time ago”. Use it only when you need to show before–after order between two past moments.

d) Participle clauses

Before / After / While + V-ing, + main clause

A phrase with before / after / while + V-ing opens the sentence, followed by the main clause. One essential condition: the subject of both parts must be the same person or thing.

After struggling with him, Susan pulled the bag from his hands.

2. Past habits

a) Past Simple

It can also describe past habits or states, but it usually needs a time expression to go with it.

I always got up at six in those days.
(a past habit)
I lived in Austria for several years.
(a past state)

b) Used to

S + used to + V  ·  negative: didn't use to  ·  question: Did … use to …?

It describes past habits or states without needing a time expression. Used to strongly suggests that the habit or state no longer exists now — it creates a contrast with the present. It is only used for the past; there is no present form.

I used to get up at six, but now I get up at eight.
I used to own a horse.
I didn't use to like beer. — Did you use to swim every day?

c) Would

S + would + V (repeated actions only)

It describes the typical, repeated actions of a person in the past; it is common in writing and in personal reminiscence. Unlike used to: would cannot describe states (you cannot say *would be a dancer to mean “used to be a dancer”), and its contrast with the present is also weaker.

Every evening was the same. Jack would turn on the radio, light his pipe and fall asleep.

d) Past Continuous + always — annoying habits

The Past Continuous with a frequency adverb (always…) can describe a repeated past action, often an annoying habit.

When Peter was younger, he was always getting into trouble.
⚠️ Apart from this use, the Past Continuous is not used for ordinary past habits or routines — that is the job of the Past Simple / used to / would.

e) Polite and tentative

The Past Continuous with think, hope, wonder makes what you say softer, more polite or more tentative.

I was thinking of having a party next week. I was hoping you would join us. I was wondering if you could help me.

⭐ Key points

  1. Past Simple: an event completed in the past (the main events when telling a story); it also describes past habits and routines.
  2. Past Continuous: (a) describes the background scene; (b) an action in progress, often contrasted with a sudden event. Not used for past routines.
  3. Participle clauses (Before/After/While + V-ing) open the main clause; the subject of both parts must be the same.
  4. Past Perfect: a past event that happened before another past event. With before/after, the Past Perfect is optional. Do not use it just because something happened “long ago”.
  5. Used to: past only; there is no present form.
  6. Would: describes habitual past actions, mainly in writing; its contrast with the present is weaker than used to; never used for states. Example: Jim would always make his mother a cup of tea after lunch (an action), but you must say Sally used to be a dancer (a state).
