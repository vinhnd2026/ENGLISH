1. The passive — when is it used?

a) Only transitive verbs can be made passive

Only verbs with an object (transitive verbs) can be turned into the passive; verbs without an object (intransitive verbs) cannot.

They sent the letter. → The letter was sent.
They arrived late.
(No passive form is possible.)

A verb with both a direct and an indirect object gives two possible passive forms:

They sent me the letter. → I was sent the letter. / The letter was sent to me.

Some transitive verbs, in some uses, still cannot be made passive — for example like and love: I like this place. (this sentence has no passive form).

b) Changing the focus and omitting the agent

Bringing the object to the front of the sentence changes what the sentence focuses on:

United were beaten by Arsenal.
(We are more interested in United.)

The passive is often used when the agent (the person who performs the action) is not important, not known, or obvious from the situation:

Students are asked not to smoke.
(An impersonal, objective statement.)
My bike has been stolen!
(The agent is unknown.)
The box was opened with a knife.
(This describes how the action was done.)

2. Special passive structures

a) Reporting verbs

S + is said / believed / known … + to V / to be V-ing / to have V₃

The passive is often used with say, believe, understand, know and similar verbs in reports, to avoid a vague subject like they / people:

People say that John Wilson lives in New York. → John Wilson is said to live in New York.
They say John Wilson is travelling in Africa. → John Wilson is said to be travelling in Africa.
People say John Wilson has arrived in Australia. → John Wilson is said to have arrived in Australia.

b) Have / Get something done

have / get + O + V₃

When you arrange for someone else to do a job for you, use have something done. Replacing have with get is more informal; get can also suggest managing to get a difficult job finished.

Last year I had new tiles put on the roof.
I'm having my hair cut this afternoon.

The same structure also describes bad luck caused by an unknown person:

Peter had his car stolen last week.

c) Need doing = need to be done

need + V-ing  (= need to be + V₃)
The floor is filthy. It needs cleaning. (= It needs to be cleaned.)

d) Other points to notice

A preposition that goes with the verb stays in the passive sentence:

People shouted at the Prime Minister during his speech. → The Prime Minister was shouted at during his speech.

Be born is a passive form, but it does not have a clear passive meaning:

I was born near Kyoto.

Make meaning “force” needs to in the passive:

They made David work hard. → David was made to work hard.

Because the agent is usually unimportant, unknown or obvious, it is usually left out. When you do want to name the agent, use by: This letter was delivered this morning (by courier).

⭐ Key points

  1. Not every verb can be made passive — check in a dictionary whether a verb is transitive or intransitive.
  2. Mention the agent only when that information is really necessary.
  3. The passive is often used to give an objective, impersonal point of view.
  4. Changing the passive into the active slightly changes the meaning — especially the focus of attention. Some contexts suit the passive, others do not.
  5. The passive tends to be used more in writing, especially in scientific and technical language.
