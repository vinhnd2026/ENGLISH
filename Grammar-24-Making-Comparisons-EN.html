1. Comparative adjectives

a) Adding -er

small → smaller  ·  early → earlier  ·  late → later

One-syllable adjectives, and two-syllable adjectives ending in -y, generally add -er to make the comparative (with y changing to i). Adjectives ending in -e add only -r.

b) Using more / less

more / less modern  ·  more / less interesting

Other two-syllable adjectives, and adjectives of more than two syllables, use more or less.

c) Both forms possible

polite → politer / more polite

A small group of two-syllable adjectives have both forms: clever, common, narrow, polite, quiet, simple, tired.

d) Irregular forms

good → better  ·  bad → worse  ·  far → farther / further

Further has two meanings: the normal meaning of “more far”, and another meaning of just “more”.

How much further do we have to go?
(more far)
I can't really advise you any further.
(more)

Old has a regular form older, and an irregular form elder that is used as an adjective:

This is my elder sister.

2. Superlative adjectives

small → smaller → the smallest  ·  early → earlier → the earliest  ·  late → later → the latest
modern → more modern → the most modern  ·  interesting → more interesting → the most interesting

Superlatives follow similar rules to comparatives: one-syllable adjectives use (the) -est and longer adjectives use (the) most. The irregular forms are:

good → the best  ·  bad → the worst

3. Comparatives of adverbs

fast → faster → the fastest  ·  early → earlier → the earliest  ·  efficiently → more / less efficiently → the most / least efficiently

Adverbs follow the same rules as adjectives: one-syllable adverbs add -er / -est, adverbs ending in -y do the same, and longer adverbs use more / less and the most / least. The adverbs well and badly are irregular:

well → better → the best  ·  badly → worse → the worst

4. Making comparisons

The simplest kind of comparison uses than:

You look younger than your brother.

Comparatives can be repeated to suggest continuing change:

This lesson seems to be getting longer and longer.
Jim started feeling more and more tired.

Comparatives can be made stronger with much, far, a lot, or weaker with a bit, a little:

This book is much more expensive.
This book is a little more expensive.

Note the construction that repeats the word the — as one side changes, the other side changes too:

The faster you drive, the more petrol you use.

Expressions with best:

Sorry, but this is the best I can do.
I tried my best.
May the best man win.
(this can refer to two or more people)

5. Comparative clauses

a) With than

Food here is more expensive than I thought.

b) With not as / so … as

Being a nurse is not as interesting as being a doctor.

c) With (just) as … as

Living in the country is just as expensive as living in London.

d) With such … as

I've never been to such a good party as that one.

e) Auxiliaries at the end of the sentence

When we compare actions, we can use an auxiliary at the end of the sentence to avoid repeating the verb. Modals like can are repeated, but other verbs use a form of do:

I can swim a lot better than Jack can.
You paid more for your car than I did.

⭐ Key points

  1. Spelling rules for comparative and superlative adjectives: in one-syllable adjectives ending with one consonant, double the final consonant: big → bigger → the biggest. In adjectives ending in -y, change y to i: happy → happier → the happiest.
  2. Adverbs form comparatives and superlatives like adjectives: fast → faster → the fastest.
  3. Auxiliaries are often used at the end of a comparative clause to avoid repeating the verb: Wendy works twice as hard as I do. (NOT … as I work)
  4. Further can mean “more far” or simply “more”; elder is only used as an adjective before a noun (my elder sister), never with than.
