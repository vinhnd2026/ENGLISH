1. Inversion after negative adverbs

In formal speech and writing there is a special construction when the sentence begins with a negative adverb (or one with negative meaning): the subject and the verb are inverted, into question form.

Negative adverb + auxiliary + S + V  ·  Not only · No sooner · Never · Seldom · Rarely · Little · Not once · Only then · Only after · Under no circumstances
Harry not only missed the train, but also lost his case. → Not only did Harry miss the train, but he also lost his case.

a) No sooner — always with the Past Perfect

With no sooner, the main verb is always in the Past Perfect, and the second part follows with than.

No sooner had Tim left than the phone rang.

b) Under no circumstances

Under no circumstances should you touch these wires.

c) Seldom / Rarely / Never / Little / Not once / Only then / Only after

Seldom have I watched a better match.
Only then did I realise that I had lost my keys.

2. Inversion to emphasise place

With some expressions we can begin the sentence with an adverb and then put the verb before the subject, for emphasis.

a) come / go + adverb of place — exclamations, informal

Here comes Jack!
There goes my money!
Up went the plane into the clouds!

b) live / stand + adverb of place — formal

In this house lived Charles Dickens.
On a hill outside the town stands the castle.

c) be with now

Now is the best time to visit the Channel Islands.

3. Question tags

Sometimes a simple statement is too short and does not help the conversation to develop in a friendly way. We can add a tag to the end of the statement so that it becomes a question — then the other person knows that they have to reply.

You're French, aren't you?

a) How tags are formed

Tags are formed using auxiliaries (do, be, have or a modal), repeating the auxiliary of the main clause.

Statement + , + auxiliary (+ not) + pronoun?
Helen lives here, doesn't she?
You left early, didn't you?
Jack was born in Italy, wasn't he?
Someone's got to pay, haven't they?

b) Positive ↔ negative

A positive statement has a negative tag, and expects the answer Yes. A negative statement has a positive tag, and expects the answer No.

You agree with me, don't you? — Yes, I do.
You don't take sugar, do you? — No, I don't.

c) Special cases

It is possible for a positive tag to follow a positive statement, to express interest or ask for confirmation. This is less common:

So you like working here, do you?

Tags with will and won't can be used after imperatives:

Don't drive too fast, will you?

Let's … has a tag formed with shall:

Let's have a drink, shall we?

⭐ Key points

  1. In formal speech and writing it is possible to use inversion after negative adverbs. The adverb has to be at the beginning of the sentence or clause.
  2. With no sooner, the main verb is always in the Past Perfect: No sooner had Tim left than the phone rang.
  3. Intonation carries important meaning in question tags: rising tags are real questions, falling tags are checking information.
  4. Positive statement → negative tag; negative statement → positive tag. Let's… → shall we?; imperatives → will you?
