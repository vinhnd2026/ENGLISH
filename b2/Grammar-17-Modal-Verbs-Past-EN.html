Grammar 17 — Modal Verbs: Past

Modal verbs — past: could / was able to · must have / can't have · may / might / could have · had to · should have · needn't have / didn't need to

1. Past ability

Could and was able to

could + V (general ability)  ·  was / were able to + V (one particular action)

To talk about general ability in the past, use could. To talk about one particular action on one specific occasion, use was able to.

When I was young, I could run very fast.
(general ability)
Luckily, Mary was able to help us.
(one particular action)

2. Deduction about the past (certainty and uncertainty)

a) Must have and can't have — logical deduction

must have + V₃ (certain that it happened)  ·  can't have + V₃ (certain that it did not happen)

These two phrases are used to make logical deductions about past events.

I must have left my wallet in the car.
(I am sure I left it there)
Jim can't have noticed you.
(I am sure he did not see you)

b) May have, might have and could have — possibly…

These phrases express possibility, or uncertainty, about past events.

Jean might have missed the train.
(perhaps she missed it)
He may not have received the letter.
(perhaps he has not received it)
You could have been killed!
(that was a real possibility)

c) Was/Were to have — planned, but it did not happen

was / were to have + V₃

This expresses something that was supposed to happen, but in fact did not. The use is formal.

He was to have left yesterday.
(he was due to leave, but he did not)

3. Past obligation

a) Had to — the past form of must

had to + V  ·  question: Did you have to + V?

Must has no past form, so we use had to.

Sorry I'm late, I had to take the children to school.

b) Should have and ought to have — criticism

should have / ought to have + V₃

These two express the feeling that a mistake has been made — they carry criticism: something should have been done but was not, or something was done that should not have been.

I should have posted this letter yesterday.
(I failed to do the right thing)
You shouldn't have told me the answer.
(what you did was wrong)

c) Needn't have and didn't need to

needn't have + V₃ (it was done, but it turned out not to be necessary)  ·  didn't need to + V (not necessary; whether it was done is not stated)

There is a small difference between the two forms: needn't have states that the action did happen but was not necessary; didn't need to does not tell us whether it happened or not. In everyday speech, didn't need to is often used for both cases.

I needn't have arrived at seven.
(I did arrive at seven, but it was not necessary)
I didn't need to arrive at seven.
(we don't know what time I arrived — at seven, or later)

4. Contractions in speech, and indirect speech

a) Have is usually contracted in speech

In spoken English, have in the forms in this lesson is usually contracted after the modal verb.

I must've left my wallet in the car.
(must've = must have)

b) Must and shall in indirect speech

In indirect speech, must is reported as must or had to; shall is reported as should.

'You must go.' → He told me I had to go.
'Shall I help?' → He asked if he should help.

⭐ Key points

  1. Could describes general ability in the past; was/were able to describes one particular action that was actually achieved.
  2. Must have + V₃: certain that it happened. Can't have + V₃: certain that it did not happen. May/Might/Could have + V₃: possible that it happened.
  3. Should have / ought to have + V₃ carries criticism — something should have been done (but was not), or should not have been done (but was).
  4. Needn't have + V₃: it was done, but it was not necessary. Didn't need to + V: it was not necessary (whether it was done or not is unclear).
  5. In speech, have is usually contracted with past modals: You shouldn't've done it.
