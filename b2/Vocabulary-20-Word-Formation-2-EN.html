1. Prefixes — use each prefix once

Review familiar prefixes in new combinations: over- (too much), in- (negative), sub- (under), auto- (self), ex- (former), mis- (wrongly), semi- (half, partly), un- (negative), non- (not), dis- (negative / disappear). When each prefix is used only once, cross out the ones you have used to find the one that is left.

auto- · dis- · ex- · in- · mis- · non- · over- · semi- · sub- · un-
Dick was very tired and suffering from overwork.
We couldn't see the magician! He had become invisible.
Let me introduce you to Janet, my ex-wife.

2. Suffixes — more word families

The suffixes in this unit: -hood, -ship and -ity make abstract nouns (neighbourhood, partnership, equality); -er makes the person or thing that does something (employer); -en turns an adjective into a verb (wide → widen); -able makes adjectives meaning “can be …” (drinkable); -ful (full of) and -less (without) are an opposite pair (handful / hopeless); -ish means “slightly” (greenish).

-hood · -er · -en · -ful · -less · -ish · -ship · -able · -ity
Carol and Andy have just moved into a new neighbourhood.
I'm a terrible card player. I'm really hopeless.
Doctors have proved that smoking is harmful.

3. Compound adjectives and compound nouns

Compound adjectives are usually joined with a hyphen; the second part often ends in -ed or -ing: kind-hearted, empty-handed, fully-clothed, long-legged, absent-minded, good-looking, loud-mouthed, short-tempered, dog-eared (about a book whose page corners are bent). Familiar compound nouns: bus station, car park, haircut; and more compound adjectives: part-time, well-dressed, old-fashioned, well-known, first class, homemade.

kind-hearted · empty-handed · fully-clothed · long-legged · absent-minded · good-looking · loud-mouthed · short-tempered · dog-eared
Thank you for helping me, and being so kind-hearted.
Mike gets angry easily. He's a bit short-tempered.
We left our car in the multi-storey car park.

4. Opposites, verb phrases and fixed preposition phrases

Many opposites are completely different words, not just a prefix: victory ↔ defeat, fresh ↔ stale, opening ↔ ending. Fixed verb phrases: make a fuss, lose touch, bear relation to, drop a line, waste time, catch sight of, think better of, gain experience, take a seat, break the news. And preposition phrases to learn by heart: by myself, by chance, on purpose, in common, under control, from now on, by surprise, on loan, for a change, in tears.

victory ↔ defeat · dangerous ↔ safe · accept ↔ decline · fail ↔ pass · take-off ↔ landing · fresh ↔ stale · opening ↔ ending
Go away. I want to be by myself.
This wasn't an accident! You did it on purpose!
Don't make such a fuss! I'll only be gone a week!

⭐ Key points

  1. When each prefix is used only once, cross out the ones you have used — even the hardest word becomes easy when only one choice is left.
  2. The suffix -en turns adjectives into verbs (wide → widen); -hood, -ship, -ity make abstract nouns; -able makes adjectives meaning “can be …”; -ful and -less are an opposite pair.
  3. Compound adjectives usually have a hyphen, and the second part often ends in -ed or -ing: long-legged, absent-minded, good-looking.
  4. Many opposites are different words, not prefixes: victory/defeat, fresh/stale, dig up/bury, opening/ending.
  5. Fixed preposition phrases must be learnt as wholes: by chance, on purpose, in common, under control, by surprise, on loan, for a change, in tears.
