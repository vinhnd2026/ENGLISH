Vocabulary 9 — Crime and the law

Crimes and criminals · Police, court and punishment · Prepositional phrases and a detective story

1. Crimes and criminals

break the law — do something illegal; commit a crime — do a crime (always use commit, not make); illegal — against the law; prohibited / banned — not allowed; compulsory — must be done.

People who break the law: a hijacker takes control of a plane or boat by force; a kidnapper takes people and demands money for their return; a blackmailer gets money by threatening to tell secrets; a smuggler brings goods into a country illegally; a forger makes illegal copies of paintings or documents; a burglar steals from houses; a pickpocket steals wallets in a crowd; a shoplifter steals from shops; a murderer kills someone on purpose; a vandal damages other people's property; a hooligan causes trouble at football matches.

A witness is a person who sees what happens during a crime or accident; an offence is a crime or illegal act (a first offence).

The two men were arrested before they could commit any more crimes.
Use commit with crime — not make or perform.
Smoking is prohibited near the petrol tanks.
The hijackers took fifteen people hostage and demanded £1,000,000 from the authorities.

2. Police, court and punishment

The process: arrest — take someone to a police station as a prisoner; charge someone with a crime — accuse them officially; a suspect — a person the police think committed a crime; the accused — the person on trial; evidence — facts that show what happened; a statement — what a witness says officially; sue — start a legal case against someone; take someone to court.

At the trial: the court, the trial itself, the judge, the jury (ordinary people who decide guilty or not), the verdict (their decision), guilty, a defence lawyer, and the case (the judge dismissed the case = stopped the trial because there was no evidence).

Punishments: a fine (money paid as punishment), a sentence (the punishment the judge gives), a life sentence, prison, corporal punishment (physical punishment), capital punishment (the death penalty), being pardoned (officially forgiven and set free), and police officers in plain clothes (ordinary clothes, not uniform).

The murderer of the children received a life sentence.
Pauline decided to sue the police because she had been wrongly accused.
Charles could not deny having been at the scene of the crime.
deny + -ing: deny having been …
If your dog damages your neighbour's property, you could be liable.
liable = legally responsible for paying for damage.

3. Phrasal verbs and set phrases

break into — enter by force; hold up — rob using threats or weapons; get away — escape; get away with — do something bad without being punished; be let off — be allowed to go without serious punishment; be taken in — be deceived or cheated; find someone guilty — decide officially that they committed the crime.

Fixed phrases: against the law, a life of crime, under arrest, in self-defence, in trouble with the police, hidden from sight, in danger, off duty, by mistake, on purpose, in secret, send (something) to.

The bank at the end of the street was held up by two masked men last week.
hold up = to rob a bank or shop using threats.
As it was Sheila's first offence she was let off with only a warning.
I regret to tell you that you are under arrest.
under arrest — a fixed phrase with under.

⭐ Key points

  1. commit a crime — always use commit (not make or perform); break the law = do something illegal.
  2. rob vs steal: you rob a person or place (robbed of their purses), but you steal a thing.
  3. Verb patterns: accuse someone of doing · charge someone with · sentence someone to · deny doing · admit/confess to doing · refuse to do.
  4. guilty (did the crime) ↔ innocent; liable = legally responsible for paying for damage; corporal punishment = physical punishment, capital punishment = the death penalty.
  5. Fixed phrases: against the law, under arrest, in self-defence, by mistake, on purpose, in secret, in danger, off duty, in trouble with.
  6. take someone to court = sue someone; get away with something = do something bad without being punished.
