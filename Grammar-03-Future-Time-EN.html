Grammar 3 — Future Time

Future time: Will · Going to · Future Continuous · Future Perfect · Present Continuous / Present Simple for the future · when / until / as soon as

1. Predicting the future

a) Will — predictions

S + will + V  ·  in speech, usually shortened to 'll

Used to make predictions. It is often introduced by I think or by opinion words such as perhaps, and it needs a time expression to go with it.

I think it'll rain tomorrow. Perhaps she'll be late.

b) Going to — predictions when we can see the cause

S + be + going to + V

Also used for predictions, and especially common when we can see the cause of something that is about to happen. It is also common in objective statements. In most of these cases, will can also be used without changing the meaning.

Look out! There's a bus coming! It's going to hit us!
You're going to fall!
Liverpool are going to win the Cup.
(an objective statement)

c) Future Continuous — in progress at a future moment

S + will + be + V-ing

It describes a situation that will be in progress at a particular moment in the future.

This time next week I expect I'll be living in London. And I'll probably be cycling to work.

d) Future Perfect — looking back from the future

S + will + have + V₃ (past participle)

We stand at a point in the future and look back: the event will already have been completed before that point. When that future moment comes, we can say it in the present perfect: The film has started.

By the time we get there, the film will have started.

2. Intentions and decisions

a) Going to — an intention we already have

It describes a present intention or plan — something we have already decided.

I'm going to fix the television tomorrow.
(already decided)

b) Will — a decision made at the moment of speaking

Used for a decision made instantly, at the very moment of speaking.

I know, I'll get him a wallet for his birthday.
(a decision made at the moment of speaking)

3. Fixed arrangements and timetables

a) Present Continuous — fixed arrangements

S + be + V-ing (with a future meaning)

It describes arrangements that are certain and fixed — usually social appointments and meetings, as written in a diary. It is also used to ask about social arrangements.

Sorry, I can't help you, I'm leaving in the morning.
(already arranged)
Are you doing anything this evening?

b) Present Simple — timetables and programmes

It describes a future event based on a timetable, programme or schedule.

My train leaves at 11.30 tomorrow morning.
(according to the train timetable)

4. Sau when, until, as soon as

After the time words when, until, as soon as, we use a present form (the present simple) even when we are talking about the future — we do not use will in that clause. To emphasise that the event will be complete, use the present perfect.

I'll … + when / until / as soon as + S + V (present simple) / have + V₃
I'll wait for you here until you get back.
I'll tell you the news when I see you.
I'll wait here until you have finished. — Call me when you have finished.
(the present perfect emphasises completion)

⭐ Key points

  1. Will and going to are both used for predictions; going to is especially common when we can clearly see the cause of something about to happen (You're going to fall!). With will there is often I think / perhaps and a time expression.
  2. Going to = an intention or plan decided in advance; will = a decision made at the moment of speaking.
  3. Future Continuous (will be + V-ing): a situation in progress at a moment in the future. Future Perfect (will have + V₃): an event completed before a point in the future.
  4. The Present Continuous is used for fixed arrangements, and to ask about social arrangements (Are you doing anything this evening?).
  5. The Present Simple is used for events in a timetable or programme (My train leaves at 11.30).
  6. After when, until, as soon as referring to the future, use the present simple or the present perfect (to emphasise completion), not will: I'll tell you the news when I see you. Call me when you have finished.
