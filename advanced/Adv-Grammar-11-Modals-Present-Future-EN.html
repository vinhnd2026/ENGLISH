Advanced · Grammar 11 — Modals: present and future

Modal verbs in the present and future: don't have to / must not · should · could / can · must / can't · may / might · shall · will / would · need · had better / be bound to

1. Obligation and advice: don't have to · must not · should

a) Don't have to and must not — opposite meanings

don't have to + verb = no obligation  ·  must not + verb = an obligation NOT to do something

Don't have to refers to an absence of obligation — you are free not to do it. Must not, in contrast, refers to an obligation not to do something — it is forbidden.

You don't have to work tomorrow.
no obligation
You must not leave the room before the end of the test.
forbidden

b) Should — wherever should appears, ought to can also be used

Expectation — what is expected to be true or to happen:

This film should be really good.

Recommendation:

I think you should talk it over with your parents.

In writing, should can express a strong obligation politely:

Guests should vacate their rooms by midday.

Criticism of an action:

You shouldn't eat so much late at night.

Uncertainty in questions:

Should I leave these papers on your desk?

With verbs of thinking, should makes an opinion less direct:

I should think that model would sell quite well.

With be and adjectives describing chance (odd, strange, funny = odd) and the expression What a coincidence:

It's strange that you should be staying in the same hotel!

After in case, to emphasise unlikelihood:

I'm taking an umbrella in case it should rain.

2. Possibility, deduction and certainty: could · can · must · can't · may · might

a) Could

Possibility or uncertainty:

This could be the house.
possibility

With comparative adjectives, could expresses possibility or impossibility:

The situation couldn't be worse.
it is impossible for it to be worse
It could be better.
possibility

Suggestions:

We could go to that new restaurant opposite the cinema.
a suggestion

Unwillingness — something you feel you absolutely cannot do:

I couldn't possibly leave Tim here on his own.

b) Can

Can with be is used to make criticisms:

You can be really annoying, you know!
a criticism

Can with be also refers to capability — what is generally possible:

Winter here can be really cold.
capability

c) Must and can't — opposites expressing certainty (present time only)

must be = I'm sure it is  ·  can't be = I'm sure it isn't
This must be our stop.
I'm sure it is.
This can't be our stop.
I'm sure it isn't.

d) May and might

Both express possibility or uncertainty; may is more common in formal language:

The peace conference may find a solution to the problem.

May in although clauses:

She may be the boss, but that is no excuse for shouting like that.

May / might as well — the only thing left to do, something the speaker is not enthusiastic about:

Nobody else is going to turn up now for the lesson, so you may as well go home.

An idiomatic expression with try — may for present reference, might for past reference:

Try as I might, I could not pass my driving test.
although I tried hard, I could not pass

3. Shall · will · would · need and related expressions

a) Shall

Shall can be used with all persons to emphasise something the speaker feels is certain to happen or wants to happen (shall is stressed):

I shall definitely give up smoking this year.
We shall win!

Shall is also used in formal rules and regulations:

No player shall knowingly pick up or move the ball of another player.

b) Will

Will can express an assumption:

A: The phone's ringing. B: That'll be for me.

Will / won't can be used emphatically to state an intention or forbid an action, in response to a will expression. Won't can mean I refuse, and will can mean I insist:

A: I won't do it! B: Yes, you will!

c) Would — an understood conditional

Would is often used where a conditional sense is understood but not stated:

Nobody would agree with that idea.
if we asked them
Life wouldn't be worth living without you.
if you weren't there

d) Need

Need to is a modal auxiliary that behaves like a normal verb:

Do you need to use the photocopier?

Need as a modal auxiliary is used mainly in question and negative forms:

Need you make so much noise?

e) Related non-modal expressions

Had better — a recommendation referring only to the present or future:

You'd better not phone her again.

Be bound to — a future prediction of certainty:

It's bound to rain tomorrow.

⭐ Key points

  1. Don't have to = absence of obligation; must not = an obligation NOT to do something — opposite meanings.
  2. Should (= ought to): expectation, recommendation, polite strong obligation in writing, criticism; it softens opinions with verbs of thinking (I should think…), follows in case, and appears with chance adjectives (odd, strange, funny).
  3. Could: possibility, uncertainty, suggestions; couldn't possibly = absolute unwillingness. Can + be: criticism or general capability.
  4. Must and can't are opposites expressing certainty in the present: must = I'm sure it is, can't = I'm sure it isn't.
  5. May / might: possibility (may is more formal); may / might as well = the only thing left to do; Try as I may / might = however hard I try.
  6. Shall emphasises certainty and appears in formal rules; will expresses assumptions (That'll be…), insistence or refusal; would carries an understood conditional; had better gives advice, be bound to predicts certainty.
