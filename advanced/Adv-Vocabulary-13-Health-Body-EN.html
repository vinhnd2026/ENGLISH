1. Keeping fit and medical care

Staying healthy has become a growth industry: people spend money on medical treatment, health foods and keep-fit clubs. A keep-fit fanatic is someone obsessed with exercise, and someone who is particular about what they eat is very careful and choosy about food.

Keeping fit has become a growth industry.
Keeping fit is now a fast-growing business area.
He is very particular about what he eats.
He takes great care over his food.
Private medical treatment can be very expensive.
Treatment outside the public system can cost a lot.

2. Tiredness, illness and injury

Adjectives for bodily states are easily confused: wide awake (fully awake) contrasts with drowsy (sleepy, often because of medicine); worn out (exhausted after effort) differs from run down (in poor health through illness or overwork). For injuries and illness: sore (painful), sprained (twisted and injured, e.g. an ankle), gasping for breath (breathing with difficulty), an attack of + illness (a sudden episode), handicapped (having a disability).

These tablets may make you feel drowsy, so don't drive.
Drowsy = sleepy and less alert.
After twelve hours at work, I feel worn out.
Worn out = completely exhausted.
Climbing the stairs left me gasping for breath.
Gasping = breathing hard and with difficulty.
She spent a week in bed with an attack of rheumatism.
An attack of = a sudden episode of an illness.

3. Word formation in health topics

Watch for common word-building patterns: verb + -iate (differentiate — to show a difference), noun of quantity (content(s) — the amount contained), products (things produced), adjective + -ous (scandalous — shocking), -ation (relaxation), a person who practises (practitioner), the negative prefix im- (imbalance — lack of balance), popular (widely believed), scientific (from science).

It's hard to differentiate bottled water from tap water.
Differentiate X from Y = show how X differs from Y.
Illness is caused by imbalance in the body.
Imbalance = a lack of balance or harmony.
Homeopathy is based on very sound scientific principles.
Scientific = based on science; sound = reliable.

4. In hospital: people, places and equipment

Hospital vocabulary: stretcher (a bed on poles for carrying patients), casualty (the emergency department), plaster cast (a hard cover protecting a broken bone), crutches (supports used under the arms when walking is difficult); porter (a worker who moves patients and supplies), surgery (the operating room / an operation), operation (a surgical procedure), ward (a room where patients stay and recover). For minor injuries: stung (pierced by an insect), allergic (having an allergy), sling (a band supporting an arm), plaster (a small adhesive dressing — not the same as a plaster cast).

He was carried off the pitch on a stretcher.
Stretcher = a carrying bed for injured people.
The doctor put a plaster cast on my leg.
A plaster cast protects a broken bone while it heals.
I was stung on the hand by a wasp.
Stung = pierced by a sting.
The doctor put my arm in a sling.
A sling supports an injured arm.

5. The body, weight and everyday health

About shape and weight: flabby (soft and fat), overweight (too heavy), figure (the shape of the body), dieting (eating less to lose weight). About diagnosis and treatment: diagnosed (identified by a doctor), insomnia (inability to sleep), fainted (lost consciousness), come round (become conscious again); at the dentist: filling (material put into a tooth cavity), extracted (pulled out), injection (a dose given with a needle), agony (extreme pain). About pregnancy: expecting a baby, pregnant, cravings (sudden strong desires for food), give birth.

The doctor diagnosed her as suffering from insomnia.
Diagnose = identify an illness.
Last week I actually fainted at work.
Faint = lose consciousness briefly.
The tooth would have to be extracted.
Extract a tooth = pull it out.
She is expecting a baby in April.
Expecting = pregnant, with the birth due in April.

6. Faces, gestures and ways of walking

Head movements and facial expressions: nod (move the head down and up — 'yes'), shake your head (move it side to side — 'no'), chuckle (laugh quietly), giggle (laugh in a silly, uncontrollable way), grin (give a large smile), frown (move the eyebrows together in disapproval), scowl (make a threatening expression), yawn (open the mouth wide when tired or bored), stare (look fixedly with wide-open eyes), choke (have trouble breathing because the throat is blocked). Verbs of walking each carry their own shade of meaning: ramble (walk for pleasure in the countryside), crawl (move on hands and knees), tiptoe (walk on the front of the foot, quietly), limp / hobble (walk with difficulty because of pain), stagger (walk unsteadily), march (walk in step, like soldiers), stroll (walk in a leisurely way), dash (move very rapidly), wander (walk with no particular purpose).

She grinned and shook her head.
Grin = smile broadly; shake your head = say no without words.
The injured player limped off the pitch.
Limp = walk unevenly because one leg hurts.
We strolled along the sea front.
Stroll = walk slowly and pleasantly.
The baby is learning to crawl.
Crawl = move on hands and knees.

⭐ Key points

  1. Health collocations: growth industry, medical treatment, keep-fit fanatic, be particular about.
  2. Bodily states: wide awake (fully awake) vs drowsy (sleepy); worn out (exhausted) is not the same as run down (in poor health).
  3. Illness and injury: sprained ankle, sore feet, gasping for breath, an attack of + illness.
  4. Hospital: stretcher, casualty, plaster cast, crutches, surgery, operation, ward, porter; note plaster (a small dressing) vs plaster cast (for broken bones).
  5. Word formation: im- + balance → imbalance; science → scientific; practice → practitioner; scandal → scandalous.
  6. Ways of walking differ in speed and purpose: stroll leisurely, dash hurried, wander aimless, limp/hobble in pain, stagger unsteadily, tiptoe quietly.
