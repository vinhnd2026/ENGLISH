1. Basic contrasts: Past Simple and Past Continuous

a) Past Simple — completed actions, habits and states in the past

S + V-ed / V₂  ·  went, bought, saw…

The Past Simple generally refers to: completed actions in the past, habits in the past, and states in the past.

I got up, switched off the radio, and sat down again.
(a series of completed actions)
Every day I went to the park.
(a past habit)
In those days, I didn't like reading.
(a state)

b) Past Continuous — actions in progress at a past moment

S + was / were + V-ing

The Past Continuous generally refers to: actions in progress (often interrupted by events); background description in narrative; changing states; and repeated actions with a sense of criticism (with a frequency adverb, similar to the use of the Present Continuous to express annoyance).

I was drinking my coffee at the time.
(in progress)
While I was opening the letter, the phone rang.
(interrupted by an event)
I entered the office and looked around. Most people were working at their desks, but Jane was staring out of the window.
(background description)
The car was getting worse all the time.
(a changing state)
When Jane was at school, she was always losing things.
(repeated action — criticism)
⚠️ The Past Continuous is not used for general habitual actions in the past (without the sense of criticism) — the Past Simple is used for that meaning: When I lived in London, I walked through the park every day.

2. Past Perfect — simple and continuous

a) Past Perfect Simple — going back to an earlier past

S + had + V₃ (past participle)

We use the Past Perfect when we are already talking about the past, and we want to go back to an earlier past time — a “double past”.

By the time I got to the station, the train had left.

Compare this with a simple statement of order. When we talk about a sequence of past events in the order that they happened, we more commonly use the Past Simple, especially with quick, short actions:

The train left five minutes before I got to the station.
⚠️ The Past Perfect is also common in reported speech. But it is not used simply to describe an event in the distant past — a distant past event still takes the Past Simple.

b) Past Perfect Continuous

S + had been + V-ing

The same contrasts between simple and continuous forms (see card 1) can be made in past perfect verb forms, for events further back in the past.

I had been living in a bed-sitter up to then.
While I had been talking on the phone, Jimmy had escaped.
The whole place was deserted, but it was obvious that someone had been living there.

3. Used to and would — past habits

a) Used to — things that were true then, but not now

S + used to + V  ·  negative: didn't use to / used not to

Used to often contrasts with the present; the contrast may be stated or understood. It can describe both repeated actions and states in the past, and it has no present-time reference.

I used to go swimming a lot (but I don't now).
⚠️ The form didn't used to is also found, but it is usually considered incorrect; use didn't use to.

b) Would — repeated actions only

S + would + V  ·  repeated actions, not states

Would is used to describe repeated actions, not states — a habitual activity which was typical of a person. It is more common in written language and often occurs in reminiscences. In many cases used to would also be possible.

Every week he'd buy his mother a bunch of flowers.
(a repeated action — would is possible)
I used to like cowboy films.
(a state — would is NOT possible here)

4. Unfulfilled past events and polite forms

a) Unfulfilled past events — intended, but they did not happen

was / were going to + V  ·  was / were about to + V  ·  was / were to have + V₃  ·  was / were thinking of + V-ing

These forms describe events that were intended to take place, but which did not happen. The contrasting past event is often understood, but not stated.

I was going to phone you, but I forgot.
I was about to do it, but I started doing something else.
Jack was to have taken part, but he fell ill.
I was thinking of going to Italy this year, but I haven't decided.

b) Polite forms — past forms to sound polite

Past forms used with wonder make a request or question sound more polite and less direct:

I was wondering if you wanted to come to the cinema.

Past verb forms are also used to express unreal time, which we will meet in later lessons. The contrasts between the Past Simple and the Present Perfect are covered in detail in the next lesson.

⭐ Key points

  1. Past Simple: completed actions, habits and states in the past.
  2. Past Continuous: actions in progress at a past moment (often interrupted), background description, changing states; with always it criticises repeated actions. It is not used for general past habits.
  3. Past Perfect = a “double past”: an event before another past event. A sequence told in chronological order usually stays in the Past Simple.
  4. Past Perfect Continuous: the same simple/continuous contrasts, one step further back in the past.
  5. Used to: for both repeated actions and states, with a contrast to the present. Would: only for repeated actions, never states; typical of reminiscence and writing.
  6. Unfulfilled past: was going to / was about to / was to have + V₃ / was thinking of — intended but not done. A past form with wonder (I was wondering if…) is a polite form.
