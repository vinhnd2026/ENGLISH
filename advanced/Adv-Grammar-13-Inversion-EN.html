1. What is inversion?

The term inversion covers two different operations that change the normal order of a clause:

a) Using a question form of the main verb

Auxiliary + S + V …
Not only did he fail to report the accident, but also later denied that he had been driving the car.
(question form of the main verb: did + subject)
Never have I enjoyed myself more!
(question form: have + subject)

b) Changing the normal positions of verb and subject

… + V + S
Along the street came a strange procession.
(verb before subject: came + a strange procession)
⚠️ Most of the inversion patterns in this unit belong to formal language. They are used for emphasis or rhetorical effect (speeches, writing), not in everyday conversation.

2. Inversion after negative adverbials

Inversion only happens when the adverbial comes at the beginning of the clause.

a) never · rarely · seldom

These are most commonly used with the present perfect or past perfect, or with modals such as can and could. Sentences of this type often contain comparatives.

Rarely can a minister have been faced with such a problem.
(with a modal: can)
Seldom has the team given a worse performance.
(with the present perfect)
Rarely had I had so much responsibility.
(with the past perfect)

b) hardly · barely · scarcely … when — no sooner … than

These refer to an event which quickly follows another in the past. They are usually used with the past perfect, although no sooner can be followed by the past simple. Note the word used in the second clause: hardly / scarcely … when, but no sooner … than.

Hardly had the train left the station, when there was an explosion.
(hardly … when)
Scarcely had I entered the room when the phone rang.
(scarcely … when)
No sooner had I reached the door than I realised it was locked.
(no sooner … than)
No sooner was the team back on the pitch than it started raining.
(no sooner with the past simple)

c) After only

Here only combines with other time expressions (only after, only when, only if, only then, only later) and is usually used with the past simple.

Only after posting the letter did I remember that I had forgotten to put on a stamp.
(only after + past simple)
⚠️ When only means 'the only one', there is no inversion after it: Only Mary realised that the door was not locked.

d) Phrases containing no / not

under no circumstances · on no account · at no time · in no way · on no condition · not until · not only … (but also)
On no condition are they to open fire without a warning.
(on no condition)
Not until I got home did I notice that I had the wrong umbrella.
(not until)

e) Little

Little also has a negative or restrictive meaning in this use:

Little does the government appreciate what the results will be.
(little = almost not at all)

3. So / such, inverted conditionals and other cases

a) So / such … that

Inversion with so + adjective (where the main verb is be) is used for emphasis; such used with be means 'so much / so great'. Inversion only happens if so / such is the first word in the clause.

So devastating were the floods that some areas may never recover.
(so + adjective + be)
Such was the force of the storm that trees were uprooted.
(such + be)

b) Conditional sentences without if

Three types of if-sentence can be inverted without if. This makes the sentences more formal and makes the event seem less likely.

Were + S + to V … · Should + S + V … · Had + S + V3 …
Were they to escape, there would be an outcry.
(= If they were to escape …)
Should you hear anything, let me know.
(= If you should hear anything …)
Had I known, I would have protested strongly.
(= If I had known …)

c) Inversion after as

This is more common in formal or written language:

We were short of money, as were most people in our neighbourhood.
(as + were)
I thought, as did my colleagues, that the recession would soon be over.
(as + did)

d) Inversion after so, neither and nor

These are used in 'echoing' statements, agreeing or disagreeing with what has just been said:

A: I am going home. B: So am I.
(agreeing with a positive statement)
A: I don't like meat. B: Neither do I.
(agreeing with a negative statement)

⭐ Key points

  1. Inversion means putting an auxiliary (or the verb itself) before the subject; it is mainly used in formal language for emphasis.
  2. After never / rarely / seldom at the start of a clause, inversion is common with perfect tenses or with can / could.
  3. hardly / scarcely … when and no sooner … than describe two past events in quick succession, usually with the past perfect.
  4. only + a time expression at the start of a clause causes inversion — but not when only means 'the only one'.
  5. Negative phrases such as under no circumstances, on no account, at no time, not until, not only … and little also cause inversion.
  6. Inversion is also used with So / Such … that at the start of a clause, in conditionals without if (Were / Should / Had), and in echoing replies with so / neither / nor.
