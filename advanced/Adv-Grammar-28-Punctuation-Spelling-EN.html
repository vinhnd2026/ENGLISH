1. Commas

Commas separate items in a list, come before question tags, separate clauses, and are placed after — and around — certain linking words such as however and for example.

I've been to Dallas, New Orleans, Kansas and Tampa Bay.
(commas separate the items in a list)
Sue is a lovely girl, isn't she?
(a comma before the question tag)
If you see Kevin, tell him his photocopies are ready.
(a comma separates the two clauses)
Broadly speaking, I agree with what you are saying.
(a comma after a linking phrase at the start)
I do not, however, agree with your last point.
(a linking word in mid-sentence — commas on both sides)
⚠️ Do not put a comma between a subject and its verb, and do not use commas in defining relative clauses:
The lady standing over there at the bus stop is my next-door neighbour.
(no comma between the subject and the verb)
Will the pupil who threw that paper dart please stand up now.
(a defining relative clause — no commas)

2. Apostrophes

Letters left out: an apostrophe shows that letters have been left out of a contracted form.

It's warm today.
(It is → It's)

Possession: apostrophes also mark possession.

Jack's car, the player's entrance, the people's decision.
(Jack's car · the player's entrance · the people's decision)
⚠️ The possessive its has NO apostrophe: write its, not it's (= it is).

Plurals of letters and figures: apostrophes are also used for the plurals of single letters and figures.

There are two I's in 'specially'.
(the plural of the letter I)
Are these 7's or 3's?
(the plurals of the figures 7 and 3)

3. Colons and semi-colons

A colon introduces examples, lists, and statements which spell out in detail what has just been stated in general:

There were two possible courses of action: borrowing from the bank, or asking for more time to find the money elsewhere.
(the two courses of action are listed after the colon)

A semi-colon divides the parts of a long sentence, or long items in a list. Very often the long sentence can simply be split into shorter sentences, so semi-colons are frequently unnecessary.

4. Spelling: words that are easy to get wrong

These words are very often misspelled — learn their correct spellings:

accommodation · address · advertisement · beginning · committee · conscience · curiosity · disappear · disappointed · embarrassed · faithfully · favourite · forbidden · government · guarantee · immediately · independent · jealous · journey · manufacture · marriage · medicine · necessary · pollution · prefer · preferred · pronunciation · quiet · quite · receive · recommend · responsibility · separate · sincerely · successful · truly · unconscious · unfortunately · unnecessary · writing

Similar spelling, different meaning:

altogether = completely · all together = (a group) all in one place
effect = (verb) bring about, make; (noun) a result · affect = have an effect on
lose = (verb) fail to have or find · loose = (adjective) not tight
specially = for a special purpose · especially = particularly
stationery = paper, envelopes, etc. · stationary = not moving
principle = a general truth or standard · principal = the head of a college or school; main

Same pronunciation, different spelling and meaning:

allowed – aloud · bear – bare · fair – fare · hair – hare · pear – pair · piece – peace · practice (noun) – practise (verb) · stair – stare · their – there · weather – whether

⭐ Key points

  1. Commas: separate list items, come before question tags, separate clauses, and go after / around linking words (however, for example…). Never between a subject and its verb, and not in defining relative clauses.
  2. A colon introduces examples, lists and detailed explanations; a semi-colon separates the parts of a long sentence — and can often be avoided by splitting the sentence into shorter ones.
  3. Apostrophes: omitted letters (it's), possession (Jack's), plurals of letters and figures (I's, 7's). Possessive its has NO apostrophe.
  4. Learn the correct spellings of commonly misspelled words: accommodation, beginning, committee, embarrassed, government, immediately, necessary, receive, recommend, separate, successful, unconscious…
  5. Distinguish similar-spelling pairs: effect/affect, lose/loose, specially/especially, stationery/stationary, principle/principal, altogether/all together.
  6. Homophones: bear/bare, piece/peace, practice (noun) / practise (verb), their/there, weather/whether… — choose by the meaning of the sentence.
