1. Parliament viewed from the outside

The opening text describes the Houses of Parliament in London: impressive from the outside, but inside the members are often absent, asleep, or shouting at each other like schoolchildren. Notice the fixed phrases (collocations) that are typical of this topic:

refer to sb as ... · a cross between A and B · complete the picture · make up the numbers · the general public · after all · go on (happen) · in any case · overall impression · to put it bluntly · behind the times · be filled with · resist · broadcast
Members refer to each other as 'The Honourable Member'.
(refer to sb as ... — the formal way members address each other)
The chamber is very rarely full when bills are discussed.
(a bill is a draft law; the chamber is the debating hall)
To put it bluntly, parliament looks disorganised and behind the times.
(to put it bluntly — speaking frankly)

Two words for people in their political role are worth separating: the electorate is the whole body of people who have the right to vote, while voters are the people who actually vote.

2. Election night: the language of elections

The election-night commentary uses a string of fixed phrases from this field:

cast a vote · hold an election · political asylum · come out of retirement · stand as a candidate · toe the party line · smear campaign · backfire · opinion poll · majority · manifesto
Almost everybody has cast their vote by 9.30 at night.
(cast a vote — to vote)
An opinion poll put the government just two per cent ahead.
(an opinion poll measures public opinion before an election)
The opposition's election manifesto contained nothing new.
(a manifesto sets out what a party promises to do)

3. People and institutions of government

a) People

president (an elected head of state) · sovereign (a head of state by birth) · minister (head of a government department) · ambassador (represents their country abroad) · delegate (represents others at a meeting) · chairperson (heads a formal meeting) · patriot (loves their country) · ringleader (leads others into trouble) · terrorist (uses violence for political ends) · traitor (betrays their country)

b) Institutions and offices

mayor · council (locally elected body) · cabinet (senior ministers) · the authorities · motion (a proposal put to a vote) · bill (a draft law) · reign (a king's or queen's period of rule) · wield power · survey / poll
The Leader of the Opposition proposed a motion of no confidence in the government.
(a motion of no confidence challenges the government's right to stay in power)
An ambassador represents their country abroad, while a delegate represents others at a meeting.
(ambassador vs delegate — abroad vs at a meeting)
The king enjoyed a long reign, and was eventually succeeded by his son.
(a reign lasts from one monarch to the next)

4. Adjectives for views and character — and the language of the law

a) People and their political and social attitudes

diplomatic (tactful) · respectable (with a good reputation) · courteous (polite) · radical (with extreme views) · oppressed (ruled unjustly) · conventional (following the usual ways) · rebellious (against authority) · privileged (with more advantages than others) · notorious (with a bad reputation) · progressive (in favour of new ideas)

b) The law: allowed, required, or forbidden?

licensed (officially allowed) · restricted (controlled, limited) · compulsory (obligatory) · barred (banned, excluded) · abolished (done away with) · binding (to be obeyed) · required (necessary) · permitted (allowed) · voluntary (by your own choice) · illegal (against the law)
If you are diplomatic, you are tactful when dealing with people.
(diplomatic — a matter of tact, not of embassies, here)
Education from the age of five is compulsory in Britain.
(compulsory — the opposite is voluntary)
Students have been barred from using local pubs since the incident.
(barred from — banned from a place)

5. One word, several jobs

Each of these five words works in three very different contexts — learn each word together with the phrases it belongs to:

following (watching closely · pursuing · after) · retains (keeps, continues to hold) · swing (a change of direction · a playground seat · a rhythm) · motion (a proposal · movement) · control (command · a restrictive measure)
Muller retains the title which he won in Sydney.
(retain a title — keep it against challengers)
Yesterday's poll shows a significant swing of public opinion away from the Democrats.
(a swing of opinion — a measurable shift)
The police were accused of heavy-handed crowd control tactics.
(crowd control — managing large groups of people)

⭐ Key points

  1. Parliament phrases: refer to sb as ..., a cross between A and B, make up the numbers, behind the times, to put it bluntly, in any case.
  2. Elections: cast a vote, hold an election, stand as a candidate, opinion poll, majority, manifesto, toe the party line, smear campaign.
  3. Heads of state: a president is elected, a sovereign is born to the role; an ambassador represents a country abroad, a delegate represents others at a meeting; a minister runs a department; a chairperson heads a meeting.
  4. Institutions: council (local), cabinet (senior ministers), the authorities, a motion of no confidence, a bill (a draft law).
  5. The law: compulsory (you must) vs voluntary (you choose); permitted vs illegal; binding = to be obeyed; abolish a law; bar sb from a place; licensed to sell regulated goods.
  6. One word, many contexts: following · retains · swing · motion · control — always learn the word with its collocations.
