1. Basic uses of the passive

S + be + past participle (V₃)  ·  the object of the active sentence becomes the subject

The passive puts the person or thing that receives the action in subject position. The verb is always be + past participle, and the tense is shown by the form of be.

a) Agent and instrument

The person who performs the action in a passive sentence is called the agent, and is introduced by by; the agent may or may not be mentioned. A thing which causes something to happen is called an instrument, and is introduced by with.

My purse was found by one of the cleaners.
(the agent is one of the cleaners)
A new road has been built.
(the agent is not mentioned)
He was hit on the head with a hammer.
(the instrument is a hammer)

b) Verbs with two objects

Verbs which have two objects can be made passive in two ways: either the indirect object (usually a person) or the direct object can become the subject.

I was handed a note. — A note was handed to me.
(two passive versions of the same sentence)

Other common verbs of this type are:

bring · give · lend · pass · pay · promise · sell · send · show · tell

c) Verbs with object and complement

Some verbs have a noun or adjective which describes their object (an object complement). When these verbs are made passive, the complement goes directly after the verb.

We elected Jim class representative. → Jim was elected class representative.
(elect + object + complement)
Everyone considered him a failure. → He was considered a failure.
(consider + object + complement)

d) Verbs which can't be passive

Most verbs with an object (transitive verbs) can be made passive: drive, for example, is transitive because one can drive something (a car). However, a few transitive verbs may not be used in the passive. These include:

become · fit · get · have · lack · let · like · resemble · suit

Verbs with no object (intransitive verbs) cannot be passive either, because there is no object to become the subject. Fall, for instance, is intransitive — you cannot “fall something” — so we cannot say *The tree was fallen; the sentence must stay active:

The tree fell.
(active only)

2. Using and not mentioning the agent

a) Change of focus

The passive can change the emphasis of a sentence: the active sentence focuses on the doer, while the passive focuses on the receiver of the action — and the agent can still be mentioned at the end.

Jack won the prize. — The prize was won by Jack.
(focus on Jack — focus on the prize)

b) Four reasons for leaving out the agent

The agent is unknown — in this case there is no point in adding “by somebody”:

My wallet has been taken.
(we do not know who took it)

The subject is “people in general”, or a general “you”:

Bicycles are widely used in the city instead of public transport.
(a generalised agent)

The agent is obvious, or has already been referred to:

Linda has been arrested!
(we assume: by the police)
The company agreed to our request and a new car park was opened.
(no need to repeat the agent)

The agent is unimportant to the meaning of the sentence:

I was advised to obtain a visa in advance.
(who gave the advice does not matter)

c) Impersonality and processes

Using the passive is a way of avoiding the naming of a specific person who is responsible for an action:

It has been decided to reduce all salaries by 10%.
(the decision-makers are not named)

In descriptions of processes, the emphasis is on the actions performed rather than on the people who perform them:

Then the packets are packed into boxes of twenty-four.
(a process description)

⭐ Key points

  1. The passive is be + past participle; the focus moves from the doer to the receiver of the action.
  2. An agent (the doer) is introduced by by; an instrument (a tool) is introduced by with — do not mix them up.
  3. Verbs with two objects (give, send, show, tell…) have two passive versions.
  4. With verbs that take a complement (elect, consider…), the complement stays directly after the passive verb.
  5. Some verbs have no passive: become, fit, have, lack, let, like, resemble, suit. Intransitive verbs cannot be passive either.
  6. Leave the agent out when it is unknown, obvious, generalised or unimportant — and for impersonality or process descriptions.
