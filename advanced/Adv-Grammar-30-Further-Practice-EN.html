Further practice — the final mixed review

This is the closing unit of the advanced grammar course: there are no new explanations, just seven large practice exercises mixing almost everything you have studied — inversion, conditionals, the subjunctive, relative clauses, linking words and more. Each exercise tests a different skill, so read its instructions carefully before you begin.

a) Finding the extra word in each line (Exercises 1 and 4)

In most lines there is one unnecessary word — either grammatically incorrect or wrong for the sense of the text. Judge each line in the context of the whole passage; if a line is correct, mark it with a ✓. Common traps: an extra preposition (many of kinds), an extra pronoun (which they are absorbed), an extra verb (make emphasise).

b) Completing a letter from notes (Exercise 2)

Read the notes, then put one or two words in each space to make a formal, accurate letter. The words you need do not occur in the notes — you have to rephrase: get books back → be returned, kept from you → withheld.

c) Sentence transformation (Exercise 3)

Use the word given, without changing it. The patterns in this exercise: inversion after a negative phrase (Under no circumstances am I…, Only when I checked did I…), It's (high) time + past simple, talk someone into doing something, and a which clause commenting on the whole sentence before it.

d) Cloze passages (Exercises 5 and 7)

Put exactly one word in each space: relative pronouns (which, who), linking words (However, despite), prepositions, and fixed phrases such as under pressure, in no way, to say the least. The structure around the gap and the meaning of the whole passage are your best clues.

e) Verbs in brackets in the correct form (Exercise 6)

Choose the form the sentence structure demands: But for your help, I would not have won… (third conditional), inversion in Never before have I seen… and Not until… did I realise…, and the set phrases Be that as it may and Try as I might.

Under no circumstances am I to be disturbed.
(inversion after a negative phrase)
But for your help, I would not have won the prize.
(but for + third conditional)
Never before have I seen such heavy snow in April.
(inversion after Never before)
Be that as it may, your behaviour is unacceptable.
(set phrase: Be that as it may)
Failure to return books may result in graduation certificates being withheld.
(result in + passive -ing form)
What the press has yet to realise is that the royals are the goose that lays the golden egg.
(a What clause as subject)

⭐ Key points

  1. Inversion: when a negative or restrictive phrase comes first (Under no circumstances, Only when…, Never before, Not until…), the auxiliary verb goes before the subject.
  2. Extra-word exercises: read each line in the context of the passage. The extra word is often a preposition, a pronoun or an unnecessary verb — and some lines are completely correct and need a ✓.
  3. Letters from notes: turn the informal wording of the notes into formal style (return → be returned; keep from → withhold).
  4. Set phrases: Be that as it may, Try as I might, to say the least, at the very latest.
  5. But for + noun = If it hadn't been for…; the main clause uses would (not) have + past participle.
  6. Cloze passages: study the structure around the gap — a preposition is usually followed by a relative pronoun (in which), a sentence-opening contrast needs a linking adverb (However), and despite is followed by a noun or noun phrase.
