1. Basic contrasts: will, going to and the present continuous

a) Will — prediction and instant decisions

S + will + V

Will is the predictive future: it states known facts or what the speaker supposes to be true about the future.

I'll be late home this evening.
(a prediction)

It can also express an assumption about the present:

That'll be Jim at the door.
(an assumption — I suppose it is Jim)

And it is used for a decision made at the moment of speaking:

I'll take this one.
(a decision made now)

b) Be going to — intentions already decided, and present evidence

S + am / is / are + going to + V

Be going to describes intentions and plans that have already been decided before the moment of speaking:

I'm going to wait here until Carol gets back.
(an intention already decided)

It is also used when the cause or evidence is present now:

Look at that tree! It's going to fall.
(the cause is evident now)

Note that decisions expressed with going to generally point to a more distant future point, not to the immediate moment as with will.

c) Present continuous — fixed arrangements

S + am / is / are + V-ing  ·  usually with a time reference

The present continuous is used for fixed arrangements, especially social and travel arrangements. It is very close in meaning to the going to future:

I am having a party next week.
(= I am going to have a party next week)

2. Future continuous and future perfect

a) Future continuous — in progress at a future point

S + will be + V-ing

The future continuous describes an event that will be in progress at a future point:

Come round in the morning. I'll be painting in the kitchen.
(in progress at that future moment)

It also describes events that will happen anyway, rather than events we deliberately arrange:

I won't bother to fix a time to see you, because I'll be calling into the office anyway several times next week.
(it will happen anyway)

In some contexts it sounds more polite than will when asking about someone's plans, and it can also refer to fixed arrangements:

Will you be going to the shops later? — The band will be performing live in Paris this summer.
(a polite question) — (a fixed arrangement)

b) Future perfect — looking back from a future point

S + will have + past participle  ·  S + will have been + V-ing

The future perfect (simple and continuous) puts us at a future point and looks back: the simple form marks an event completed by then, and the continuous form marks one that has continued up to then:

In two years' time I'll have finished the book. — By the end of the month, I'll have been working for this firm for a year.
(completed by then) — (continuing up to then)

It is also used for an assumption about what has already happened:

You won't have heard the news, of course.
(an assumption: I suppose you have not heard it)

3. Other ways of referring to the future

a) be to · be about to · be on the point of · be due to · just

Be to describes formal arrangements:

All students are to assemble in the hall at 9.00.
(a formal arrangement)

Be about to and be on the point of both refer to the next moment:

I think the play is about to start now. — Mary is on the point of resigning.
(the next moment)

Be due to refers to scheduled times:

The play is due to start in five minutes. — Ann's flight is due at 6.20.
(scheduled times)

Just — with the present continuous, or in just about to — also describes something on the point of happening:

Hurry up! The train is just leaving.
(on the point of happening)

b) Present simple and present perfect in future time clauses

In future time clauses (after when, as soon as and similar words), we use the present simple instead of will; the present perfect can be used instead when we want to emphasise that the event is completed first:

When we get there, we'll have dinner. — When we've had a rest, we'll go out.
(present simple) — (present perfect, completion emphasised)

The present simple is also used for fixed events that are not simply the speaker's wishes, and for calendar references:

Tom retires in three years. — Christmas is on a Tuesday next year.
(a fixed event) — (a calendar reference)

c) hope, verbs of thinking + will, and shall

Hope can be followed by either a present or a future verb form:

I hope it doesn't rain. — I hope it won't rain.
(present form) — (future form)

Most verbs of thinking — think, believe, expect, doubt — can be followed by will when there is future reference:

I expect the train will be late. — I doubt whether United will win.
(verbs of thinking + will)

Shall for the first person in future reference is generally considered a feature of British English: it is formal in tone and possibly declining in use.

⭐ Key points

  1. Will: prediction and assumption (including about the present), and decisions made at the moment of speaking. Going to: plans already decided, and events with clear present evidence. Present continuous: fixed arrangements, usually with a time reference.
  2. Future continuous (will be + V-ing): in progress at a future point; events that will happen anyway; a more polite way of asking about plans.
  3. Future perfect: completed by a future point (will have + past participle) or continuing up to it (will have been + V-ing); also an assumption about the past.
  4. be about to / be on the point of = the next moment; be due to = scheduled times; be to = formal arrangements.
  5. In future time clauses (when, as soon as…), use the present simple — or the present perfect to emphasise completion — not will.
  6. hope can be followed by a present form or by will; verbs of thinking (think, believe, expect, doubt) take will for future reference; first-person shall is formal British English.
