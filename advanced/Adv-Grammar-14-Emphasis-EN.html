1. Changing word order to change focus

a) Passive sentences

Passive constructions vary the way information is given in a sentence, putting more emphasis on what comes first.

All roads to the north have been blocked by snow.
(the emphasis falls on “all roads”)

b) Fronting and inversion

Fronting means putting first, for emphasis, a phrase or clause that would not usually come first. Inversion goes further and puts the verb before the subject, so that a prepositional phrase is emphasised before the verb.

Suddenly down came the rain!
Where the money is coming from, I don't know.

Time phrases can also vary in position, and are often put first because the time reference is important:

At six o'clock Monica decided to phone the police.

c) Inverted may clauses

A clause with although … may … can be inverted into a shorter form. It is a highly formal expression:

Although it may seem difficult, …  →  Difficult as / though it may seem, …
Difficult as it may seem, it is not impossible.

2. Cleft and pseudo-cleft sentences

a) Cleft sentences with it is / it was

The pattern It is / It was + emphasised part + who / that + … can emphasise different parts of the sentence — the subject, the time, the object, even a reason with because. Modal auxiliaries are also possible. In speech, stress and intonation also identify the emphasis.

It was Sue who borrowed my bike.
(emphasis on the person)
It was last night that Sue borrowed my bike.
(emphasis on the time)
It was because I felt ill that I left.
It can't have been the same book that you read.

b) Pseudo-cleft sentences with what and all

Clauses beginning with what are common with verbs such as need, want, like, hate. Events can be emphasised using auxiliary do / did. Clauses beginning with all emphasise “the only thing”.

What I hate is rainy weather.
What you need is a holiday.
What Peter did was leave the windows unlocked.
All I need is another £15.

3. Adding words for emphasis

a) Own, very and indeed

Own intensifies possessive adjectives. Very can be used emphatically to mean exactly / precisely, and very … indeed intensifies adjectives.

It was my own idea.
At the very same moment, the telephone rang.
It was very cold indeed.

b) Emphasising negatives

Ways of emphasising not include at all, in the least, really. In the least / slightest usually adds bit if used before an adjective. No and none can be emphasised by at all and whatsoever.

I wasn't interested in the slightest.
There were no tickets left whatsoever.

c) The and question words ending in -ever

The can emphasise uniqueness — it is heavily stressed in speech. Question words ending in -ever add an air of disbelief to the question.

Surely you are not the Elizabeth Taylor, are you?
Whatever are you doing?

d) Auxiliary do

Do can emphasise the verb, and is stressed in speech. It is also used in polite forms.

I do like this film! It's really great!
Do sit down!
(polite form)

4. Emphasising adverbs and adjectives, and other means

a) Common emphasising adverbs and adjectives

I actually went inside one of the Pyramids.
It is by no means certain that the match will take place.
Her performance was sheer magic!
This book is utter nonsense!

b) Only with absolute adjectives

Used in this way, absolutely, completely, quite, utterly, simply, just are only possible with adjectives which express an absolute opinion (non-gradable adjectives), such as fantastic, impossible, useless, wonderful, right.

It was absolutely fantastic!
The third exam question was quite impossible.

c) Repetition and echoing phrases with so

Emphasis can also come from repetition: time phrases (day after day, time and time again, over and over again, day in, day out), repetition of the main verb, or using a possessive pronoun instead of repeating the noun. Echoing phrases with so express agreement.

David reads the same book over and over again!
I tried and tried, but it was no use.
Theirs was a successful marriage.
(Theirs stands for Their marriage)
A: This is the book you are looking for. B: So it is!

⭐ Key points

  1. Changing word order (passive, fronting, inversion) puts the emphasised part first; may clauses have a formal inverted form: Difficult as it may seem…
  2. Cleft sentences (It was X who / that…) emphasise one part of the sentence — a person, a time, a thing, even a reason with because; pseudo-cleft sentences use What… clauses and All… (“the only thing”).
  3. Own, very, indeed intensify meaning; negatives are emphasised with at all, in the least / slightest, whatsoever.
  4. Auxiliary do emphasises the verb (I do like it!) and makes polite forms (Do sit down!).
  5. Emphatic absolutely, utterly, quite, simply… are only used with absolute adjectives (fantastic, impossible, useless).
  6. Repetition (over and over again, tried and tried) and echoing phrases with so (So it is!) also add emphasis.
