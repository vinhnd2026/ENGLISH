Advanced · Grammar 27 — Linking words and phrases

Text organisers: adding points · contrast · reasons and results · generalising · concession

1. Adding and developing a point

a) Adding a point — as well as, in addition to

as well as / in addition to + noun / V-ing  ·  not only ... but also ...

To tack extra information onto what has just been said, use as well as or in addition to + a noun (or an -ing form). A stronger pattern is not only ... but also ...; when not only comes at the start of the sentence, the first clause is inverted, like a question.

As well as the obvious dangers, there was the weather to be considered.
(adding a point)
Not only were there the obvious dangers, but there was also the weather to be considered.
(inversion after not only)

b) Developing a point

besides · furthermore · in addition · moreover · what's more · on top of that · to make matters worse

These open a new sentence and pile another point on top of the previous one. On top of that and to make matters worse usually add a negative point, making the situation sound even worse.

What's more, smoking has been directly linked to lung cancer.
(developing the point)

2. Contrast

a) Free-standing contrast linkers

however · nevertheless · all the same · none the less · still

These link two sentences with opposite content. Their position is quite flexible: at the start of the sentence, inside it (set off by commas), or at the end.

The identity of the attacker is known to the police. However, no name has been released.
(however at the start)
The identity of the attacker is known to the police. No name has, however, been released.
(however inside the sentence)

b) Concession inside one sentence

(al)though / even though / while / despite the fact that + clause

Unlike the group above, these conjunctions pack two contrasting facts into one sentence: one clause concedes a fact, the other states the opposite of what you would expect.

Although the identity of the attacker is known to the police, no name has been released.
(concession in one sentence)

c) Correcting an impression and comparing

On the contrary is used when the listener has got the wrong idea: the speaker denies the previous point and states the exact opposite. In contrast sets two different things side by side, while whereas compares two parallel facts inside one sentence. As opposed to contrasts two alternatives.

No, I didn't say the President got it wrong. On the contrary, I think he's handled the affair superbly.
(correcting a wrong impression)
I prefer city life, whereas John prefers country life.
(comparing two parallel facts)
Donahue established his reputation as a novelist. In contrast, his new book is a non-fiction work.
(setting two things side by side)

3. Reasons, results and generalisations

a) Introducing a result

as a result · consequently · accordingly · thus · hence · for that reason · in turn

When one event leads to another, the result is introduced by one of these. In turn describes a chain reaction: one thing leads to another, which in turn leads back or onwards.

The government does not intend to cause any further provocation. As a result, all troops have been withdrawn.
(a result)
The employers have promised to investigate these complaints, and we in turn have agreed to end the strike.
(in turn — a chain reaction)

b) Making generalisations

broadly speaking · generally speaking · on the whole · by and large · to a large / some / a certain extent

The first group opens a general judgement about the whole picture. To a large / some / a certain extent, by contrast, shows that the judgement is only true up to a point.

By and large, this has been an encouraging year for the company.
(a general judgement)
The country's economy depends to a large extent on the tourist industry.
(true to a large extent)

4. Starting, side information, concession and frankness

a) Starting an argument

for a start · first of all · in the first place · for one thing ... and secondly ...

These introduce the first reason in a list; the following reasons are linked with secondly and so on.

That's absolute rubbish! For a start, it was Rod who said that, not me.
(the first reason)

b) New or side information

incidentally · by the way

These slip in a piece of side information that is not part of the main point — an afterthought added in passing.

By the way, do you remember an old friend of ours called Ransom?
(side information)

c) Conceding without changing your mind

even so · all the same · having said that

The speaker accepts that the other side has a point, but their own conclusion stays exactly the same.

OK, so you two have had a few problems. Even so, I don't see why you need to split up.
(conceding, same conclusion)
Lancaster is a man of great personal integrity. Having said that, I don't think he'd make a good chairman.
(qualifying the praise)

d) Frankness and "in any case"

to be (perfectly) honest · to tell the truth · at any rate · anyway

To be honest and to tell the truth warn the listener that a frank opinion is coming. At any rate and anyway leave the debatable details aside and return to the one certain, most important point.

To tell the truth, I can't stand films like that.
(a frank opinion)
Anyway, you're safe now — that's the main thing!
(back to the main point)

⭐ Key points

  1. Adding: as well as / in addition to + noun; develop a point with besides, furthermore, moreover, what's more; on top of that / to make matters worse add (usually negative) extra points.
  2. Contrast: however, nevertheless, all the same move around freely; although / even though / despite the fact that join two clauses in one sentence.
  3. On the contrary corrects a wrong impression (denying the previous point); in contrast and whereas compare two things; as opposed to contrasts two alternatives.
  4. Results: as a result, consequently, hence, thus, for that reason; in turn marks a chain reaction.
  5. Generalising: by and large, on the whole, broadly speaking = "in general"; to a large / some extent = true up to a point.
  6. For one thing / for a start open an argument; incidentally / by the way add side information; even so / having said that concede without changing the conclusion; to be honest / to tell the truth signal frankness.
