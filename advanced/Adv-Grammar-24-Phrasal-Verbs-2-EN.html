1. Phrasal verbs with GIVE and GO

This unit (like the phrasal verb units before and after it) does not go over the basics again. Instead it focuses on multiple meaning: one phrasal verb can have several quite different meanings, and some verbs appear in fixed expressions. Learn each meaning together with its own example.

give away — betray someone or reveal a secret or true identity

His false identity papers gave him away.
(give away = betray, reveal)

give off — send off a smell, gas or liquid

The cheese had begun to give off a strange smell.
(give off = send out a smell)

give out — be exhausted, come to an end (money, supplies)

When our money gave out we had to borrow some.
(give out = run out, be used up)

give over — abandon or devote (time) to something; (colloquial) stop it!

The rest of the time was given over to playing cards.
(give over = devote time to)
Why don't you give over! You're getting on my nerves.
(give over = stop — colloquial)

give up — surrender; be given up for lost: be believed dead or lost

The escaped prisoner gave herself up.
(give up = surrender)
After ten days the ship was given up for lost.
(be given up for lost)

go back on — break a promise

The management has gone back on its promise.
(go back on = break)

go in for — make a habit of, like doing; enter a competition

I don't go in for that kind of thing.
(go in for = like, do as a habit)
Are you thinking of going in for the race?
(go in for = enter a race)

go off — become bad (food, milk)

This milk has gone off.
(go off = become bad)

go on — happen (usually something bad or strange)

Something funny is going on.
(go on = happen)

go round — be enough for everyone

There weren't enough life-jackets to go round.
(go round = be enough)

go through with — complete a promise or plan, usually unwillingly

When it came to actually stealing the money, Nora couldn't go through with it.
(go through with = carry out to the end)

grow on — (colloquial) become more and more liked

This new record is growing on me.
(grow on = become liked)

2. Phrasal verbs with HANG · HAVE · HIT · HOLD

hang onto — (colloquial) keep something, not get rid of it

I think we should hang onto the car until next year.
(hang onto = keep)

have it in for — be deliberately unkind to someone (also have got it in for)

My teacher has (got) it in for me.
(have it in for = be unkind to)

have it out with — talk frankly to someone in order to settle a problem

I put up with the problem for a while but in the end I had it out with her.
(have it out with = settle by talking)

have someone on — (colloquial) deceive someone as a joke

I don't believe you. You're having me on.
(have someone on = deceive, tease)

hit it off — (colloquial) get on well with someone from the first meeting

Mark and Sarah really hit it off at the party.
(hit it off = get on well)

hit upon / hit on — discover by chance (often an idea or solution)

They hit upon the solution quite by chance.
(hit upon = find by chance)

hold out — offer (especially hope)

We don't hold out much hope that the price will fall.
(hold out = offer hope)

hold up — delay; use as an example or model

Sorry I'm late, I was held up in the traffic.
(hold up = delay)
Jack was always held up as an example to me.
(hold up = use as a model)

hold with — agree with (an idea or way of behaving)

I don't hold with the idea of using force.
(hold with = agree with)

3. Phrasal verbs with KEEP · LAY · LET · LIVE · LOOK

keep up — continue at the same level or speed

Well done! Keep up the good work!
(keep up = continue, maintain)

lay down — state a rule officially (especially lay down the law)

The company has laid down strict procedures for this kind of situation.
(lay down = state as a rule)

let down — disappoint someone, break a promise to them

Sorry to let you down, but I can't give you a lift today.
(let down = disappoint)

let in on — allow someone to know a secret or join a secret plan

We haven't let Tina in on the plans yet.
(let in on = share a secret with)

let off — excuse someone from punishment, punish lightly

As Dave was young, the judge let him off with a fine.
(let off = punish lightly)

let on — (colloquial) reveal a secret, give information away

We're planning a surprise for Helen, but don't let on.
(let on = reveal a secret)

live down — make people forget a shameful reputation — usually negative: never live it down

If City lose, they'll never live it down.
(live down = be forgiven/forgotten)

live up to — reach an expected standard

The play quite lived up to my expectations.
(live up to = reach a standard)

look into — investigate a problem

The police have promised to look into the problem.
(look into = investigate)

look on — consider, regard (usually with as)

We look on this town as our real home.
(look on … as = consider)

look someone up — visit someone when you are in the area where they live

If you're passing through Athens, look me up.
(look up = visit)

4. Phrasal verbs with MAKE · MISS · OWN · PACK · PAY · PICK · PIN · PLAY · POINT · PULL · PUSH

make for — result in, help to produce a result

The power steering makes for easier parking.
(make for = result in)

make off with — steal something and run away with it

The thief made off with a valuable necklace.
(make off with = steal and escape)

make out — pretend; manage to see, hear or understand

Tim made out that he hadn't seen the No Smoking sign.
(make out = pretend)
I couldn't quite make out what the notice said.
(make out = manage to read)

make someone out — understand someone's behaviour or character

Janet is really odd. I can't make her out.
(make out = understand a person)

make up — invent a story or excuse

I think you made up the whole story!
(make up = invent)

make up for — compensate for something bad or missing

Our success makes up for all the hard times.
(make up for = compensate)

miss out — fail to include; (colloquial) lose a chance

You have missed out a word here.
(miss out = fail to include)
Five people got promoted, but I missed out again.
(miss out = lose a chance)

own up — (colloquial) confess (usually with to)

None of the children would own up to breaking the window.
(own up = confess)

pack in — (colloquial) stop an activity, give up a job

John has packed in his job.
(pack in = give up)

pay back — (colloquial) take revenge on someone

She paid him back for all his insults.
(pay back = take revenge)

pick up — (colloquial) improve

The weather seems to be picking up.
(pick up = improve)

pin someone down — force someone to give a clear statement or decision

I asked Jim to name a suitable day, but I couldn't pin him down.
(pin down = force to decide)

play up — behave or work badly (machines); be naughty

The car is playing up again. It won't start.
(play up = work badly)

point out — draw attention to a fact

I pointed out that I would be on holiday anyway.
(point out = draw attention to)

pull off — manage to succeed in something difficult

It was a tricky plan, but we pulled it off.
(pull off = succeed)

push on — (colloquial) continue with some effort

Let's push on and try to reach the coast by tonight.
(push on = continue)

5. Phrasal verbs with PUT

put across — communicate ideas so that people understand them

Harry is clever but he can't put his ideas across.
(put across = communicate)

put down to — explain the cause of something, attribute to

Diane's poor performance was put down to nerves.
(put down to = explain by)

put in for — apply for a job or position

Sue has put in for a teaching job.
(put in for = apply for)

put oneself out — take trouble in order to help someone

Please don't put yourself out making a meal. A sandwich will do.
(put oneself out = take trouble)

put off — discourage or upset someone, make them lose concentration

The crowd put the gymnast off, and he fell.
(put off = discourage, distract)

put up — offer someone accommodation

We can put you up for a few days.
(put up = give a bed to)

put up with — tolerate, bear something unpleasant

I can't put up with all this noise!
(put up with = tolerate)

⭐ Key points

  1. One verb, many meanings: the same phrasal verb can have completely different meanings — hold up = “delay” and “use as an example”; make out = “pretend” and “manage to see or understand”. Use the context to choose.
  2. Fixed expressions with it: have it in for, have it out with, hit it off, live it down — the word it is a fixed part of the expression and cannot be replaced by another object.
  3. Register: many verbs in this unit are colloquial — have someone on, own up, pack in, push on, grow on — more natural in speech than in formal writing.
  4. Often passive: be held up (be delayed), be given up for lost, be put down to (be explained by).
  5. The particle is part of the meaning: go back on (break a promise), live up to (reach a standard), put down to (attribute to), look on … as (consider) — learn the whole expression, not the verb alone.
