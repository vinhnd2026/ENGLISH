Phrasal verbs 3: the run, set, stand, take and turn families, and their different meanings

1. The run family and rip off

rip off

charge far more than something is worth (informal).

You paid £50? They really ripped you off!
(= they charged you far too much)

run down

a) criticise someone again and again; b) lose power, or be allowed to decline.

She's always running down her husband.
(= criticising)
I think the batteries are running down.
(= losing power)

run into

meet someone by chance.

Guess who I ran into at the supermarket!
(= met by chance)

run to

have enough money to pay for something.

I don't think we can run to a holiday abroad this year.
(= afford)

run over · run through

check something quickly.

Let's run over the plan once more.
(= check)

run up

let a bill get longer and longer without paying it.

I ran up a huge telephone bill at the hotel.
(a bill left unpaid)

run up against

encounter something, usually a problem.

We've run up against a slight problem.
(= encountered)

2. The see, send and set family

see off

go to the station, airport, etc. to say goodbye to someone.

I went to the station to see them off.
(= say goodbye at the station)

see through

realise the truth about someone or something.

I saw through his intentions at once.
(= realised the truth)

send up

make fun of someone by imitating them.

Jean is always sending up the French teacher.
(= imitating to make fun)

set about

start working on something.

We must set about re-organising the office.
(= start working)

set in

become established, especially the weather, and seem likely to continue.

I think this rain has set in for the day.
(= become established)

set out

a) give in detail in writing; b) arrange; c) start an action with a clear intention.

This document sets out all the Union demands.
(= gives in detail)
Sue set out to write a biography but it became a novel.
(= started with the intention)

set up

establish something, such as a committee or an inquiry.

An inquiry into the accident has been set up.
(= established)

set (up)on

attack someone.

We were set upon by a gang of hooligans.
(= attacked)

3. The sink, slip, sort, stand, step and stick family

sink in

be realised slowly (informal, intransitive).

Slowly the realisation that I had won began to sink in.
(= be slowly realised)

slip up

make a mistake (informal).

Someone slipped up and my application was lost.
(= made a mistake)

sort out

find a solution to a problem (informal).

Don't worry, Mary will sort out your problems.
(= find a solution)

stand by

keep to an agreement or a promise.

The company agreed to stand by its original commitment.
(= keep to)

stand for

a) represent initials; b) tolerate.

E.g. stands for exempli gratia, it's Latin.
(= represents)
I will not stand for this kind of behaviour in my house!
(= tolerate)

stand in for

take someone's place.

Carol has kindly agreed to stand in for Graham at the monthly meeting.
(= take the place of)

stand up to

resist, or bear stress without breaking.

The engine won't stand up to the strain.
(= bear the stress)

step down

resign (informal).

The Chairman has stepped down after criticism from shareholders.
(= resigned)

step up

increase something.

Production at the Leeds plant has been stepped up.
(= increased)

stick up for

defend yourself or your rights (informal).

You must learn to stick up for yourself.
(= defend)

4. The take, talk and turn family, and the rest

take in

deceive someone.

Don't be taken in by her apparent shyness.
(= deceived)

take (it) out on

make someone else suffer because of one's own sufferings.

I know you are unhappy, but don't take it out on me!
(= make me suffer for it)

take off

imitate someone (informal).

Dave takes off the Prime Minister really well.
(= imitates)

take on

a) acquire a new characteristic; b) do something extra.

She has taken on too much with a full-time job as well.
(= accepted extra work)

take out

sign an insurance agreement.

Ann has taken out life insurance.
(= signed an insurance agreement)

take over

gain control of something.

The army tried to take over the country.
(= gain control)

take to

develop a liking for someone.

You'll soon take to your new boss, I'm sure.
(= start liking)

take up

occupy time.

The meeting took up a whole morning.
(= occupied)

talk into · talk out of

persuade someone into doing something, or dissuade them from it.

Paul talked me into going skiing, against my better judgement.
(= persuaded me into)

tell off

scold someone (informal).

Our teacher told us off for being late.
(= scolded)

tie in with

be in agreement with something.

I'm afraid your party doesn't quite tie in with our arrangements.
(= fit in with)

track down

trace the whereabouts of someone or something.

The police tracked down the killer and arrested him.
(= traced and found)

try out

test something, such as a machine.

Let's try out the new washing machine.
(= test)

turn down

reject an offer.

Another company offered me a job but I turned them down.
(= rejected)

turn out

a) happen to be in the end; b) come to a meeting or form a crowd.

He turned out to be an old friend of Helen's.
(= proved to be in the end)
Thousands of fans turned out to welcome the team.
(= came to form a crowd)

turn up

a) be discovered by chance; b) arrive, often unexpectedly.

Don't worry about that missing book, it's bound to turn up sooner or later.
(= be discovered by chance)
Not many people turned up for the lesson.
(= arrived)

wear off

lose effect, especially a drug.

These painkillers wear off after about two hours.
(= lose effect)

work out

calculate; work out at is used for specific amounts.

The hotel bill worked out at over £500.
(= came to a total of)

⭐ Key points

  1. Many phrasal verbs have several unrelated meanings — learn each meaning with its context: run down (criticise / lose power), set out (give in detail / start), stand for (represent initials / tolerate), turn out (prove to be / attend).
  2. With separable phrasal verbs, a pronoun object goes in the middle: turn him down, told her off, rip you off — never after the particle.
  3. Rip off (informal) = charge too much; take in = deceive; send up and take off both mean imitate in order to make fun of someone.
  4. Turn out = prove to be in the end; turn up = be discovered by chance, or arrive unexpectedly — do not confuse them.
  5. Talk someone into something = persuade them to do it; talk someone out of something = persuade them not to do it.
  6. Work out = calculate; work out at + amount = come to a total. Stand by = keep a promise; go back on = break a promise.
