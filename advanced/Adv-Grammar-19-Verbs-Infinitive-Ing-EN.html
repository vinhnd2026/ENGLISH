1. Either form — but the meaning changes

One group of verbs can be followed by -ing or by the infinitive with to, but the two forms have different meanings. This is the most error-prone group, so learn the contrasts in pairs.

a) forget / remember — things to do and memories

With to, both verbs refer to an obligation — something that has to be done (looking forwards). With -ing, they refer to past events (looking back). Both verbs can also be followed by a that clause.

I had to phone the office, but I forgot to do it.
(an obligation — the call was never made)
I don't remember learning to walk.
(a past event — a memory)

b) try — attempting and experimenting

With to, try refers to something attempted, which might fail or succeed. With -ing, it refers to making an experiment, or to a new experience.

I tried to warn him, but it was too late.
(an attempt)
Try taking an aspirin. You'll feel better.
(an experiment)

c) go on — continuing and moving on

With -ing, go on refers to the continuing of the same action. With to, it refers to moving to the next point, for example in a speech.

She went on working even though it was late.
(the same action continued)
The Prime Minister went on to praise the Chancellor.
(the speech moved to a new point)

d) mean — intending and involving

With the meaning intend, mean is followed by to. With -ing and an impersonal subject, it refers to what is involved. A that clause is possible when a meaning is being explained.

Sorry, I meant to tell you about the party.
(intend)
If we catch the early train, it will mean getting up at 6.00.
(involve)

e) regret — announcements and past regrets

With to, regret refers to the speaker's regret about what is going to be said — it often occurs in formal statements. With -ing, it refers to a regret about the past. A that clause is also possible.

We regret to inform you that your application has been unsuccessful.
(a formal announcement)
I regret saying that to him.
(a regret about the past)

f) stop — purpose and ending

With to, stop refers to an intention — pausing in order to do something else. With -ing, it refers to the ending of an activity.

Jane stopped to check the oil level in the engine.
(she paused in order to check)
The baby has stopped waking up during the night now.
(the activity has ended)

2. Either form — little or no difference in meaning

a) can't bear, hate, like, love, prefer

These verbs take both forms. Note that like to usually refers to habitual preferences, and not like to can mean think it wrong to.

We like to go out to lunch on Sunday.
(a habitual preference)
I don't like to disturb colleagues at home.
(I think it would be wrong to)

b) attempt, begin, continue, intend, plan, propose, start

With these verbs there is no difference in meaning whether we use -ing or the infinitive with to. Intend, plan and propose can also be followed by a that clause, which may include should.

3. Verbs with an object: -ing or infinitive with to

a) admit

Admit can be used with or without to before -ing. A that clause is also possible.

They admitted (to) being members of the gang.
He admitted that he was wrong.

b) allow, forbid, permit

With an object + to, these verbs say who may or may not do something. For an activity in general, with no personal object, use the -ing form.

The school forbids students to smoke in the classrooms.
(object + to)
The school does not allow smoking.
(-ing, no personal object)

c) consider

With an object + to, consider refers to an opinion. With -ing, it means think about. With a that clause, it also refers to an opinion.

She is considered to be the finest pianist of her generation.
(an opinion)
At one point I considered emigrating to Canada.
(think about)

d) imagine

Imagine takes an object + to, or an (object) + -ing; with a that clause it means suppose.

I imagined the castle to be haunted.
I couldn't imagine (her) living in a place like that.

e) require

With an object + to, require says what someone must do. With -ing, it means needs doing.

They required him to fill out a form.
(object + to)
These letters require typing.
(= need typing)

f) hear, see, watch + object

Followed by the infinitive without to, the action is presented as complete. Followed by -ing, the action is still in progress.

We watched all the cars cross the finishing line.
(the complete action)
I heard someone coming up the stairs.
(the action in progress)

4. Verbs normally followed by the infinitive with to; the bare infinitive

a) Verbs normally followed by the infinitive with to

agree · appear · arrange · ask · attempt · choose · dare · decide · demand · deserve · expect · fail · happen · hasten · hope · hurry · learn · long · manage · neglect · offer · pay · plan · pledge · pretend · promise · refuse · resolve · seek · seem · struggle · swear · threaten · vow · want · wish

Appear, (so) happen and seem are only used impersonally with a that clause:

It appears that I've made a mistake. — It so happens that he is my brother! — It seems that Mary is going to win.

Want can be used colloquially with -ing, with a similar meaning to need:

The car wants cleaning.
(= needs cleaning)

b) The bare infinitive: help, make, let

Help can be used with or without to. Make and let take an object + infinitive without to — but in the passive, make is followed by to.

I helped George (to) carry the bags.
They made me leave. — I was made to leave.
(the passive takes to)
They didn't let me leave. Let me go!

5. Verbs normally followed by -ing; verbs with an object and to

a) Verbs normally followed by -ing

appreciate · avoid · can't stand · contemplate · delay · deny · detest · dislike · endure · enjoy · escape · excuse · face · fancy · feel like · finish · give up · involve · it's no good / use · keep on · leave off · look forward to · mention · mind · miss · postpone · practise · put off · resent · risk · spend / waste time · suggest

Appreciate is often followed by a possessive + -ing. Involve has an impersonal subject.

I appreciate your trying to help.
(possessive + -ing)
Being an athlete involves regular training.
(impersonal subject)

b) Verbs followed by an object and to

advise · assist · beg · bribe · command · dare · employ · enable · encourage · instruct · invite · lead · order · persuade · select · send · teach · tell · train · urge · warn

Dare is used with to when there is an object; without an object, to can be dropped.

They dared him to jump. — I didn't dare (to) say anything. — How dare you speak like that to me!

⭐ Key points

  1. forget / remember + to = something to be done; + -ing = a memory of the past. try + to = attempt; + -ing = experiment. stop + to = pause in order to; + -ing = give up an activity. go on + -ing = continue the same action; + to = move to the next point.
  2. mean + to = intend; + -ing = involve. regret + to = be sorry to announce; + -ing = be sorry about the past.
  3. like / love / hate / prefer / can't bear and attempt / begin / continue / intend / plan / propose / start take both forms with little or no difference in meaning.
  4. allow / forbid / permit: object + to when there is a personal object, -ing when there is not. admit, consider and imagine can also take that clauses.
  5. hear / see / watch + object + bare infinitive = the whole action; + -ing = the action in progress. make / let + object + bare infinitive — but the passive of make takes to.
  6. Many verbs in the lists can also be followed by a that clause (agree, decide, hope, admit, suggest…); colloquial want + -ing means need.
