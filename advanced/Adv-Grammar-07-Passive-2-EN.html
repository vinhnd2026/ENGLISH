1. Have / get something done and need doing

a) Have / get + object + past participle — arranging for someone else to do something

have / get + object + past participle

This structure typically describes a service that someone else performs for us: we arrange for it to be done rather than doing it ourselves. It can also describe something unfortunate that happens to someone.

I've just had my car serviced. I have it done every winter.
(a service performed for us by someone else)
We had our car broken into last month.
(something unfortunate that happens to someone)

b) When get is preferred to have

Get is more likely than have in three situations: when there is a feeling that something must be done; when there is a feeling of eventually managing to get something done; and in orders and imperatives.

I really must get my hair cut.
(a feeling that something must be done)
I eventually got the car fixed at the Fast Service garage.
(eventually managing to do something)
Get your hair cut!
(an order / imperative)
⚠️ Do not use get in the present perfect passive, where it would be confused with have got (possession).

c) Need doing — needing to be done

need + V-ing  =  need to be + past participle

The need to have a service done can also be expressed with need doing: the subject is the person or thing that needs the action done to it.

Your hair needs cutting.
(= needs to be cut)

2. Passive get and reporting verbs

a) Get instead of be in the passive

get + past participle

In spoken language, get can be used instead of be to form the passive, with a stronger sense of something happening to the subject.

Martin got arrested at a football match.
(get instead of be — spoken language)

b) Reporting verbs: be + past participle + infinitive

With verbs such as believe, know, say and think (reporting verbs), a passive construction is often used to avoid a weak subject such as people or they, and to present the idea as a generalised opinion, not attached to anyone in particular.

c) Present reference — the present infinitive (to + verb)

S + be + thought / believed / known / said + to + verb
The criminal is thought to be in hiding in the London area.
(present reference — present infinitive)
Vitamin C is known to be good for treating colds.
(present reference — present infinitive)

d) Past reference — the past infinitive (to have + past participle)

S + be + thought / believed + to have + past participle
Smith is believed to have left England last week.
(past reference — past infinitive)

e) Past reporting verb

If the reporting verb is in the past, the past infinitive tends to follow it too — though not always when the verb be is used.

People thought Sue had paid too much. → Sue was thought to have paid too much.
(past reporting verb — past infinitive)
The thief was thought to still be in the house.
(with be, the present infinitive can still be used)

3. Passive and continuous infinitives, verbs with prepositions, by and with

a) Past reference with two objects — the passive infinitive

S + be + thought / known + to have been + past participle
Everyone knows the portrait was painted by an Italian. → The portrait is known to have been painted by an Italian.
(past reference with two objects — passive infinitive)

b) Continuous infinitives

to be + V-ing  ·  to have been + V-ing
Mary is thought to be living in Scotland.
(present continuous infinitive)
The driver is thought to have been doing a U-turn.
(past continuous infinitive)

c) Prepositional verbs: the preposition stays at the end

When a prepositional verb is made passive, the preposition is kept, and it ends up at the end of the sentence.

Somebody broke into our house. → Our house was broken into.
(the preposition stays at the end)

d) By and with

With is used after participles such as filled, packed, crowded, crammed. The difference between by and with may involve the presence of a person: by suggests an accident, while with suggests someone holding the object.

The train was packed with commuters.
(with after packed)
Dave was hit by a branch. — Dave was hit with a branch.
(by: an accident — with: a person hit him with one)

e) Make takes to in the passive

make + O + verb  →  be made + to + verb
My boss made me work hard. → I was made to work hard by my boss.
(make is followed by to in the passive)

f) Cover, surround, decorate…

Verbs such as cover, surround and decorate can be used with with or by. Cover can also be followed by in.

The furniture was covered in dust.
(cover + in)
The living room had been decorated with flowery wallpaper.
(decorate + with)

4. Common contexts for the passive

The passive is probably more common in written English, where there tends to be less personal reference — partly because the audience may be unknown.

A reminder of the points from Passive 1: the passive is used to change the focus of the sentence, to avoid generalised subjects (people, somebody…), and to make an action impersonal. It is common in descriptions of processes, and in scientific and technical language in general.

⭐ Key points

  1. Have / get + object + past participle: you arrange for someone else to do a service for you; also used for misfortunes. Get is preferred for urgency, for eventual success, and in imperatives — but avoid get in the present perfect passive.
  2. Need doing = need to be done: Your hair needs cutting.
  3. In speech, get can replace be in the passive: got arrested, got broken into…
  4. Reporting verbs: present → to + verb (is thought to be); past → to have + past participle (is believed to have left); past with two objects → to have been + past participle; in progress → to be + -ing / to have been + -ing.
  5. Prepositional verbs keep their preposition at the end of the passive sentence (was broken into). Distinguish by (agent / accident) from with (an instrument in someone's hand; also after packed, filled, crowded, crammed). Cover can also take in.
  6. Make needs to in the passive: was made to work. The passive is especially common in formal written English, process descriptions and scientific or technical texts.

👁 Model answer (for reference)

It is not known exactly when gunpowder was invented. It is known for a fact that rockets and fireworks were made by the Chinese long before gunpowder was used in Europe, which occurred at about the beginning of the thirteenth century. It is generally believed that the 'Age of Chivalry' was brought to an end by gunpowder, since a mounted knight could be brought down by anyone with a firearm. In fact, efficient firearms were not developed until the sixteenth century. When it was first introduced, gunpowder was used mainly in siege cannon. Later it was used in engineering work and in mining, but it was found to be extremely dangerous. Gunpowder has now been replaced by modern explosives, but it is still used for making fireworks, just as it was by the Chinese.
