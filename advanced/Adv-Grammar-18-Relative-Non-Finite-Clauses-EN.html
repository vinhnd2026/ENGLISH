1. Defining and non-defining relative clauses

a) Defining clauses

A defining clause tells us exactly which person or thing we mean. It is closely attached to the noun it describes and cannot be separated from it.

By 4.30, there was only one painting which hadn't been sold.
(the clause says which painting)

b) Non-defining clauses

A non-defining clause only adds extra information. In writing it is separated by commas, and in speech it is usually shown by intonation.

By 4.30, which was almost closing time, nearly all the paintings had been sold.
(extra information about the time)

2. Which and that

In a defining clause, which and that can be used interchangeably, although which is felt to be more formal.

By 4.30, there was only one painting that hadn't been sold.
(that instead of which)

⚠️ That is not used to introduce a non-defining clause:

The train, which was already an hour late, broke down again.
(non-defining — which is required)

⚠️ That cannot follow a preposition — which is used in that position:

It was a service for which I will be eternally grateful.
(for which, not *for that)

In everyday speech, that is often used instead of who in defining clauses:

Do you know the girl that lives next door?
(that instead of who)

3. Who, whom and whose

a) Whom — the formal object form of who

Whom is the object form of who and is used formally when the relative pronoun is an object:

He was a person whom everyone regarded as trustworthy.
(formal object form)

However, most speakers now feel this is excessively formal, and who is commonly used instead. Whom is obligatory in only one position: immediately after a preposition.

To whom it may concern. — To whom am I speaking?
(very formal — whom after the preposition)

In everyday use, this kind of construction is usually avoided, and the preposition goes at the end:

Who am I speaking to?
(the everyday alternative)

b) Whose — possession

Whose means “of whom” and is used in both defining and non-defining clauses:

Several guests, whose cars were parked outside, were waiting at the door.
(non-defining)
Several guests whose rooms had been broken into complained to the manager.
(defining)

4. When and where

a) Non-defining: after a named time or place

Come back at 3.30, when I won't be so busy.
(after a specific time)
I stopped in Maidstone, where my sister owns a shop.
(after a named place)

b) Defining: after general time and place words

When follows words such as time, day, moment; where follows words such as place, house, street:

There is hardly a moment when I don't think of you, Sophia.
(when after moment)
This is the street where I live.
(where after street)

5. Omitting the relative pronoun and reducing clauses

a) Omitting the relative pronoun

The relative pronoun is commonly omitted in defining object clauses, especially in everyday conversation:

I've found the keys (which/that) I've been looking for.
(object pronoun omitted)
That's the man (who/that) I was telling you about.
(object pronoun omitted)

b) Sentences ending in a preposition

Another very common feature of conversational English is to end a defining clause with a preposition or phrasal verb:

That's the house I used to live in.
(preposition at the end)
I couldn't remember which station to get off at.
(phrasal verb at the end)
He's not someone who I really get on with.
(phrasal verb at the end)

c) Omitting who/which + be

A verb phrase after who/which can be reduced to an adjectival phrase, especially to define phrases such as the only one, the first/last one:

Jim was the only one of his platoon not taken prisoner. (= who had not been taken prisoner)
(reduced clause)
By 4.30, there was only one painting not sold. (= which had not been sold)
(reduced clause)

d) Which commenting on the whole situation

A non-defining clause with which can comment on the whole situation described in the main clause. Common fixed phrases: at which point/time, in which case, by which time, in which event.

There was nobody left on the train, which made me suspicious.
(which = the whole situation)
I watched the play until the end of the first act, at which point I felt I had seen enough.
(at which point)
A warning sign 'Overheat' may come on, in which case turn off the appliance at once.
(in which case)

e) Clauses beginning with what, whatever, whoever, whichever

What means “the thing or things which” and can start a clause. Whatever, whoever, whichever are used in a similar way:

I can't believe what you told me yesterday.
(what = the thing that)
What you should do is write a letter to the manager.
(what-clause as subject)
You can rely on Helen to do whatever she can.
(whatever)
Whoever arrives first can turn on the heating.
(whoever)

6. Non-finite clauses with an -ing form

These are clauses without a main verb (reduced clauses). The examples here are non-defining, and the two clauses have the same subject.

a) Actions happening at the same time

Waving their scarves and shouting, the fans ran onto the pitch.
(two actions at the same time)

b) One action happening before another — often giving the reason

This type of clause often explains the reason for something; it can also begin with on or upon:

Opening the letter, she found that it contained a cheque for £1,000.
(first action, then result)
Realising there was no one at home, I left the parcel in the shed.
(the reason)
On opening the letter, she found that it contained a cheque for £1,000.
(with on + -ing)

c) An event which is the result of another event (having + past participle)

I didn't get wet, having remembered to take my umbrella.
(having remembered = because I had remembered)

d) Passive constructions shortened to a past participle

Where a passive construction might be expected, it is often shortened to a past participle:

Having been abandoned by his colleagues, the Minister was forced to resign.
(passive, with having been)
Abandoned by his colleagues, the Minister was forced to resign.
(passive, shortened further)

⭐ Key points

  1. A defining clause identifies which person or thing we mean and cannot be separated from the noun; a non-defining clause adds extra information and is separated by commas.
  2. Which and that are interchangeable in defining clauses, but that does not introduce non-defining clauses and cannot follow a preposition.
  3. Whom is the formal object form of who — obligatory after a preposition; whose shows possession and works in both clause types.
  4. An object relative pronoun in a defining clause is often omitted, and prepositions commonly go at the end of the clause in speech.
  5. Who/which + be can be reduced (especially after the only one, the first/last one); which can also comment on a whole situation: at which point, in which case…
  6. Non-finite -ing clauses: simultaneous actions, an earlier action (often the reason, sometimes with on/upon), a result (having + participle), and passives shortened to a past participle.
