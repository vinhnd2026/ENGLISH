1. Talking about past events

a) Past Simple — main events, finished

S + V-ed / V₂  ·  went, bought, saw…

1. Basic contrasts: Present Simple and Present Continuous

a) Present Simple — facts, habits and states

S + V  ·  add -s / -es for the third person singular

The Present Simple is generally used for: facts that are always true, habits (things that happen repeatedly) and states (continuing situations, not actions in progress).

Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius.
(a fact)
British people drink a lot of tea.
(a habit)
I don't like gangster films.
(a state — a preference)

b) Present Continuous — actions in progress

S + am / is / are + V-ing

The Present Continuous refers to actions that are in progress around the moment of speaking, in three ways: they can be temporary; they can be actually in progress right now; or they can be generally in progress but not actually happening at this moment.

I'm staying in a hotel until I find a flat.
(temporary)
The dog is sleeping on our bed!
(actually in progress now)
I'm learning to drive.
(generally in progress)

2. State verbs and event verbs

a) State verbs — normally no continuous form

State verbs describe a continuing state, not an action, so they do not usually have a continuous form. Typical examples are:

believe · belong · consist · contain · doubt · fit · have · know · like · love · matter · mean · need · own · prefer · seem · suppose · suspect · understand · want · wish
⚠️ We say I know the answer, not *I am knowing the answer.

b) One verb, two meanings: state and event

Some verbs have a stative meaning and a different active meaning: be, depend, feel, have, measure, see, taste, think, weigh. Only the active meaning can be used in the continuous. Compare each pair:

Jack is noisy. — Jill is being noisy.
State: noisy by nature. Event: behaving noisily at the moment.
Deirdre has a Porsche. — We're having an interesting conversation!
State: owns. Event: experiencing.
I think I like you! — David is thinking about getting a new job.
State: an opinion. Event: considering.
This fish tastes awful! — I'm just tasting the soup.
State: has a flavour. Event: the action of tasting.
I feel that you are wrong. — I'm feeling terrible.
State: an opinion. Event: a feeling at the moment.
This bag weighs a ton! — We're weighing the baby.
State: has a weight. Event: the action of weighing.
It depends what you mean. — Bill, I'm depending on you to win this contract for us.
State: is determined by. Event: relying on.

Note: these state / event differences apply to all verb forms, not just to present verb forms.

3. Other uses of the present tenses

a) Present Continuous — more uses

Temporary situations:

Are you enjoying your stay here?

Repeated actions in the current period (not necessarily at this moment):

My car has broken down, so I am walking to work these days.

Complaints about annoying habits, with always, constantly, continually, forever:

You are always making snide remarks about my cooking!

Change and development:

The weather is getting worse! More and more people are giving up smoking.

b) Present Simple — more uses

Making declarations, and verbs describing opinions and feelings (these tend to be state verbs):

I hope you'll come to my party. I bet you don't know the answer! I hereby declare this hospital open!

Headlines — written in a “telegram” style, where references to the past are usually simplified to the Present Simple:

Ship sinks in midnight collision.

Instructions and itineraries — instructions and recipes can be written in the Present Simple instead of imperatives (a more personal style); itineraries describe travel arrangements:

First you roll out the pastry. — On day three we visit Stratford-upon-Avon.

Summaries of events — plots of stories and films, and summaries of historical events, use present (and present perfect) forms:

May 1945: The war in Europe comes to an end.

The “historic present” — in informal speech, past events can be described in the present to make the narration seem more immediate and dramatic, especially in stories and jokes:

… So then the second man asks the first one why he has a banana in his ear …

⭐ Key points

  1. Present Simple: facts that are always true, habits, and continuing states.
  2. Present Continuous: actions in progress around now — temporary situations, things actually in progress, or things generally in progress.
  3. State verbs (know, like, belong, want, seem…) describe states, so they normally have no continuous form.
  4. Some verbs have two meanings, state and event (think, have, taste, weigh, feel, be, depend, measure, see) — only the event meaning can be continuous.
  5. The Present Continuous is also used for repeated actions in the current period, complaints with always / constantly / continually / forever, and change and development.
  6. The Present Simple is also used in declarations, headlines, instructions and itineraries, summaries of plots and historical events, and the “historic present” in storytelling.
