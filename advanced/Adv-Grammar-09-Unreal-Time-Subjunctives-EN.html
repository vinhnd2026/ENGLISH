Advanced · Grammar 9 — Unreal time and subjunctives

Unreal time and subjunctives: It's time · wish / if only · I'd rather · as if · suppose / imagine · the subjunctive

1. It's time and wish — verb forms “one step back” for unreal time

a) It's time / it's high time + Past Simple

It's time / It's high time + S + Past Simple / Continuous

After It's time and it's high time we use a past verb form, even though the time referred to is the present and the event has not happened yet — this is “unreal time”.

It's time we left.
(we have not left yet)
It's high time I was going.
(long past the time to go)

b) wish — changing a present or future state

wish + S + Past Simple / Past Continuous

After wish the verb also moves one step back: wishes about changing a present or future state use past forms, although the time referred to is not the past.

I wish I had a motorbike.
(I don't have one now)
I wish you weren't leaving.
(you are leaving)
I wish I was going on holiday with you next week.
(I am not going)

c) wish + would — wanting somebody or something else to change

wish + S (somebody / something else) + would + V

Would is used after wish when the speaker wants somebody or something else to change. This use is also common for describing an annoying habit.

I wish he would change his mind and marry Jane.
(wanting someone else to change)
I wish it would stop raining.
(wanting the weather to change)
I wish you wouldn't make such a mess.
(an annoying habit)

d) wish — regrets about the past

wish + S + Past Perfect (had + V3)

For wishes about a past event which cannot be changed, the verb form moves one stage further back, to the Past Perfect. This use of wish is common after if only to express regrets.

I wish I hadn't eaten so much.
(a regret about the past)

2. hope, I'd rather and I'd prefer

a) hope — different from wish

Wishes about simple future events (things that can perfectly well happen) are expressed with hope, followed by a present form or will — not by the “one step back” forms of wish.

I hope it doesn't rain tomorrow.
(a simple future wish — hope)
I hope you'll have a lovely time in Portugal.
(a good wish for the future)

b) I'd rather / I'd sooner followed by a clause

I'd rather / I'd sooner + S + Past Simple …

When I'd rather (or I'd sooner) is followed by a clause about somebody else's actions, the verb in the clause moves one step back, as after wish. But when nouns or phrases are simply being compared, normal verb forms are used.

I'd rather you didn't smoke in here.
(preference about someone else's action)
I'd rather be a sailor than a soldier.
(comparing nouns — normal form, present)
I'd rather have lived in Ancient Greece than Ancient Rome.
(comparing nouns — normal form, past)

c) I'd prefer — the object it is needed before a clause

I'd prefer it if + S + Past Simple …

I'd prefer can be used in the same way as I'd rather, but note that prefer in this type of sentence has the object it. In other situations, I'd prefer is not followed by an unreal verb form.

I'd prefer it if you didn't go.
(prefer + it + clause)
I'd prefer tea to coffee.
(no unreal form here)

3. as if / as though and suppose / imagine

a) as if / as though — real and unreal

After as if / as though, the verb form depends on whether the situation is true (normal form) or unreal (one step back). Note, however, that the more colloquial like does not require this verb form change.

You look as if you're having second thoughts.
(true — he is having second thoughts)
He acts as if he were in charge.
(unreal — he isn't in charge)
I feel as if an express train had hit me.
(unreal — it didn't hit me)
You look like you've just seen a ghost. — You look as if you'd just seen a ghost.
(like: no change of form; as if: one step back)

b) suppose / imagine — understood conditions

After suppose and imagine, the conditional part of the sentence is often understood but not stated, and the verb moves back as in conditional sentences. But if the event referred to is a real possibility rather than imaginary, a present verb form is possible.

Imagine we won the pools!
(imaginary)
Imagine we'd never met!
(imaginary — we have met)
Suppose it starts raining, what'll we do?
(a real possibility — present form)

4. The subjunctive

a) Formal subjunctives after verbs of demanding, insisting, etc.

demand / insist / suggest / require … + that + S + base form (be for all persons)

After verbs such as demand, insist, suggest, require which involve an implied obligation, the subjunctive may be used in formal style. It has only one form, that of the infinitive — there is no third person -s and no past form. The verb be has the form be for all persons.

They demanded that he leave at once.
(leave — not leaves)
The school Principal suggested that he be awarded a scholarship.
(be — not is)

b) Less formal usage

Less formally, should can be used; and colloquially no verb form change is made, or an infinitive construction is used.

They demanded that he should leave.
(with should)
They demanded that he left.
(informal — no change of form)

c) Formulaic subjunctives

Some fixed expressions still use the subjunctive:

God save the Queen! — Be that as it may … — Come what may …
(fixed expressions)

⭐ Key points

  1. After It's time / it's high time use a Past Simple / Continuous form, although the time is present: It's time we left.
  2. After wish the verb moves “one step back”: present wishes → Past Simple (I wish I had…); past wishes → Past Perfect (I wish I hadn't eaten…).
  3. wish + would is used when the speaker wants somebody or something else to change, often about an annoying habit: I wish you wouldn't make such a mess.
  4. Simple future wishes use hope (+ present / will), not wish: I hope it doesn't rain tomorrow.
  5. I'd rather + clause moves back like wish; I'd prefer needs it: I'd prefer it if you didn't go. Comparing nouns takes normal verb forms.
  6. as if / as though: true → normal form, unreal → one step back; suppose / imagine work like understood if-clauses.
  7. The formal subjunctive: after demand, insist, suggest, require… use the uninflected base form (be for all persons); should is the less formal alternative.
