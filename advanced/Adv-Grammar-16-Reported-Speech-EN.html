1. Natural verb forms and modal auxiliaries

a) The basic principle: use the form that is natural

The most important rule in reported speech at this level is to choose verb forms that are natural in the situation, rather than mechanically putting every verb one stage further back. If what is reported is still true now, a present form can stay; past forms that are already complete stay as they are too.

‘I’m happy to help you,’ she said. → She told me she is happy to help us.
(still true now, so no backshift)
‘I wanted to go to the cinema, but John wasn’t so keen,’ said Sue. → Sue said that she wanted to go to the cinema, but John wasn’t so keen.
(the past forms in the original stay unchanged)

b) Modals after a past reporting verb

Where a modal auxiliary has a ‘past’ equivalent, it changes after a past reporting verb:

will → would  ·  can → could  ·  may → might

Could, would and might are already past forms, so they do not change.

‘I might be late.’ → She said (that) she might be late.
(might stays the same)

c) Should and must

Should changes to would only when it is a first-person form of would; in other cases it stays as should. Must can be reported as had to, or left unchanged as must.

‘I should love to come.’ → She said (that) she would love to come.
(here should is a first-person would, so it changes)
‘You should rest.’ → They said (that) I should rest.
(should of advice stays unchanged)

2. Conditionals in reported speech and transferred negation

a) Real situations

After a past reporting verb, conditional sentences about real situations change their verb forms in the usual way:

‘If we leave now, we’ll catch the train.’ → I told him that if we left then we’d catch the train.
(leave → left, will → would, now → then)

b) Hypothetical situations

If a hypothetical event can still happen in the future, the verb forms do not change (only time words such as tomorrow → the next day change):

‘If you came back tomorrow, I’d be able to help you.’ → She said that if I came back the next day, she’d be able to help me.
(came back stays, because the return is still possible)

If the event is clearly hypothetical and impossible, the forms move one stage further back:

‘If I had a spanner, I could fix it.’ → He said that if he had had a spanner he could have fixed it.
(had → had had, could fix → could have fixed)

Hypothetical past conditionals do not change.

c) Transferred negation — ‘don’t think’

With verbs of thinking such as think, expect and suppose, the negative can be transferred from the reported statement to the reporting verb itself:

I suppose she won’t come. = I don’t suppose she’ll come.
(both versions mean the same)

3. Reporting verbs and their patterns

a) Verb + that-clause

A large group of reporting verbs is followed by a that-clause (* means the verb can also be followed by a person as object):

add · admit · agree · announce · argue · believe · claim · complain · confirm · consider · decide · deny · doubt · estimate · expect* · explain · feel · hope · imply · insist · mention · object · persuade* · predict · promise* · reassure* · remark · repeat · reply · report* · say · state · suggest · suppose · tell* · think · threaten · warn*

b) Verb + person + to

advise · ask · forbid · instruct · invite · order · persuade · remind · tell · warn

c) Verb + should, or the subjunctive

Verbs which carry the sense that someone ‘should do’ something — suggest, demand, insist, recommend, propose, request, urge, advise, prefer, agree — can be followed by should. More formally, the subjunctive can be used instead: the base form of the verb, with no third-person -s.

They suggested that she should apply again.
(with should)
They suggested that she apply again.
(the subjunctive — formal)

d) Verb + that + would, or verb + to

Verbs which report statements containing will (decide, expect, hope, promise, threaten) can be followed by that + would, or by a to-infinitive:

‘I’ll leave at 8.00.’ → She decided to leave at 8.00. / She decided (that) she would leave at 8.00.
(both patterns are possible)

4. Function verbs, action verbs and changes of viewpoint

a) Verbs describing a function

Many verbs do not report the actual words; they describe the function of what was said — whether the speaker was advising, inviting, reminding, warning and so on: admit, advise, agree, complain, invite, persuade, remind, request, suggest, threaten, warn.

‘Look, if I were you I’d leave early.’ → She advised me to leave early.
(advice → advised)

b) Verbs describing actions

Some verbs describe a whole communicative action: accept, congratulate, decide, greet, interrupt, introduce.

‘Hi, Dave, how are you?’ → He greeted me.
(a greeting → greeted)

c) Changes of viewpoint

In reported speech, references to time, place and person are no longer clear in the way they are between two people in the same place, so they have to be made specific, or replaced by a more general reference:

‘I left the parcel on this chair.’ → He said he had left the parcel on the chair by the window.
(this chair → the chair by the window: which chair is made clear)
‘I love this town.’ → She said that she loved the town.
(this town → the town)

⭐ Key points

  1. The golden rule: choose the verb form that is natural in the situation — if the content is still true, there is no need to backshift.
  2. After a past reporting verb: will → would, can → could, may → might; could / would / might never change.
  3. Should becomes would only when it is a first-person form of would; must becomes had to or stays as must.
  4. Conditionals: real situations backshift; possible hypotheticals stay unchanged; impossible hypotheticals move one stage further back; past hypotheticals never change.
  5. With think / expect / suppose, the negative can transfer to the reporting verb: I don’t suppose she’ll come.
  6. Learn each reporting verb’s pattern (that-clause, person + to, that + should / subjunctive), and adjust time, place and person references to the new viewpoint.
